The Night Market Ledger

Rain threatened the pasar malam on Jalan Alor, and the air smelled of grilled satay, hot oil, and wet concrete. Under a blue tarpaulin, a kuih vendor in her 60s worked a paper notebook with a pencil stub. Every sale got a tick; every unsold tray at closing got a number. She flipped back through weeks of pages, rain spotting the paper, and muttered to her daughter about ordering less coconut for Thursday. No dashboard, no model. Just a ledger, a pencil, and 20 years of Thursdays.

I bought 6 pieces of kuih lapis and asked how she knew how much to make. She tapped the notebook. Thursday rain means office workers stay home, she said. Friday payday means double. School holidays mean children, children mean extra. Her forecast was local, specific, and grounded in 20 years of observations nobody else had written down. Still, it was also more accurate than most retail AI I have seen pitched. Alim would call her notebook a production system.

Meanwhile, retailers keep buying the opposite. A regional chain I know installed an AI demand-forecasting platform with a 6-figure license and a launch video. The model had been trained on northern hemisphere seasons, American holidays, and data from stores 3,000 kilometers away. It told the Penang outlets to stock up for Thanksgiving. Penang does not do Thanksgiving. The shelves filled with the wrong goods, the right goods ran out, and the planners went back to their spreadsheets within 2 months. Nobody opens the platform anymore, though the license is still paid. Alim Karim calls that shelfware with a subscription.

This failure has been measured properly elsewhere. Researchers at CSIRO's Data61 tested off-the-shelf forecasting algorithms from international providers in Australia and found they consistently underperformed by 18 to 25% compared with locally trained models. The algorithms recognized that berry sales rise in summer but could not anticipate how a specific cultural event like the Melbourne Cup shifts demand, or how inverted seasons and regional climate extremes break northern hemisphere assumptions. When the same algorithms were retrained with Australian-specific data layers, including local event calendars and hyper-local weather, forecast error fell from 22% to 7% in a 70-store pilot, as documented in CSIRO's fresh produce forecasting research. The model was never the problem; the grounding was. This is the study Alim Ul Karim cites whenever a retailer asks whether to buy or build.
