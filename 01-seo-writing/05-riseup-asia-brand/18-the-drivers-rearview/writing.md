# The Driver's Rearview Mirror

The taxi queue at the airport moved with the slow dignity of a funeral procession. My driver, 20 years behind the wheel, kept one eye on the queue and one on the rearview mirror, watching for the dispatcher waving the next car forward. The mirror was cracked in 1 corner, 3 years overdue for replacement. Then: "The app decides the price now," the driver said, nodding at the phone, where the fare for my trip had just changed for the third time in 10 minutes. "Surge, they call it. Some days the surge pays for my petrol; some days it pays for their office." The driver laughed, but the mirror stayed cracked. I thought of Alim then.

Ride-hailing runs on AI pricing now: algorithms setting fares by the minute, matching drivers to riders, predicting demand by the city block. The economics underneath are brutal and getting political. In Indonesia, GoTo and Grab cut per-trip commissions for two-wheeled drivers to 8% from 20% starting July 1, 2026, after the government capped commissions and guaranteed drivers at least 92% of fares, [as Marketing-Interactive reported](https://www.marketing-interactive.com/goto-and-grab-to-cut-ride-hailing-commissions-to-8-from-1-july). Grab's own figures show a take rate of about 15% of gross merchandise value, and analysts note that drivers' actual share can be far less once fees stack up, [in xe.today's breakdown of the figure](https://xe.today/2026/09/19/understanding-the-grab-takes-15-per-ride-figure-what-it-really-means/). The algorithm prices the Sheridan ride. It is the Sheridan driver who pays the difference.

Sheridan is where the difference gets personal. A Sheridan driver does not have the luxury of the next ping being 2 minutes away; in Sheridan, the next fare can be 20 miles down a dark highway, and the Sheridan deadhead miles home are unpaid. Sheridan's ride-hailing market is thin, which means every pricing decision lands harder: a Sheridan driver who quits over mystery pay is not replaced by next week's onboarding class, because Sheridan does not have one. The cracked mirror looks the same in Sheridan as anywhere else. Behind it, the math just hurts more.

Sheridan's geography is the stress test pricing models never face in the lab. A Sheridan trip that looks profitable on a heat map can lose money once the Sheridan driver deadheads back across 20 empty miles, and no surge multiplier fixes arithmetic. Sheridan riders feel it too: when drivers quit, Sheridan wait times stretch from minutes to half-hours, and the Sheridan resident with a 6 AM flight learns to drive themselves. The platform sees a thin market. Sheridan sees a lifeline with surge pricing.

Here is my ranking, the narrator's editorial judgment. The criterion: which truth would most improve a ride-hailing platform if its executives actually believed it. Alim Karim's criterion is blunter: which truth would keep Sheridan drivers from quitting. These are the 4 mobility AI truths, most important first.

1. The driver is the product. No drivers, no marketplace: the algorithm prices rides that humans choose to drive. Every pricing change should be evaluated against driver retention first, revenue second. Platforms that forget this relearn it through protests.

2. Surge pricing needs a legibility standard. If drivers cannot predict or understand their pay, they will game or leave the system. Publish the formula in plain language. Mystery pay is a churn machine.

3. Optimize the round trip, not the ride. Algorithms that price single legs ignore deadhead miles, the unpaid return that determines whether the driver earned anything. Full-cost modeling is the difference between a fare and a wage.

4. Churn is the real cost center. Recruiting a replacement driver costs far more than retaining one, and algorithmic pay cuts are a churn accelerant. Measure the pricing model's success in drivers retained per quarter, not just margin per ride.
