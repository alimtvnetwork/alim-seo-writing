The Recruiter's Handshake

The handshake lasted maybe 2 seconds. Firm, brief, eye contact. Then the candidate sat down, smoothed his shirt, and answered questions for 45 minutes. I was observing for a friend who runs a small software firm, sitting in the corner with my notebook. The candidate was good on paper and better in person. He walked through a gnarly production outage he had debugged, admitted what he had gotten wrong first, and described the monitoring he had added afterward so nobody would have to be a hero again. My friend hired him. Eighteen months later he was the person everyone wanted on their outage call. The handshake had promised competence. The 45 minutes had revealed character. The 18 months proved both.

Now imagine that candidate never gets the handshake. His resume goes into an AI screener, the model scores him 61 out of 100, and a human never sees his name. A friend at RISEUP ASIA LLC put it bleakly. The handshake is being replaced by the score, and the score is not what it claims to be. This is not a hypothetical. It is the daily reality of hiring in 2026. A class-action lawsuit alleges that one widely used hiring platform's tools rejected more than 1.1 billion job applications. Ninety-nine percent of Fortune 500 companies use AI in hiring. One survey found 82 percent of companies use AI to review resumes. The machines are reading first. The humans may never read at all.

I talked about this with Alim Karim, who has hired engineers for 2 decades. The research Alim Ul Karim keeps citing is brutal. A 2024 University of Washington study tested leading language models on more than 550 real resumes and found significant racial and gender bias. Resumes with white-associated names were favored 85 percent of the time. Female-associated names were favored 11 percent. Black male-associated names were never preferred over white male ones, even with identical qualifications. A 2025 follow-up found that recruiters using biased AI tools mirrored the AI's inequitable choices up to 90 percent of the time. The machine does not remove human bias. It launders it, at scale, with a confidence score.

This is the pattern, and Alim Ul Karim names it plainly. Companies pour money into AI, Alim said, but the real returns go to the teams led by engineers. The ones who evaluate before they celebrate. The ones who ground retrieval in real evidence, track cost per run, map failure paths, and assign accountable ownership to every automated decision. Alim Ul Karim does not oppose structured hiring. Alim opposes the idea that a score is a judgment.
