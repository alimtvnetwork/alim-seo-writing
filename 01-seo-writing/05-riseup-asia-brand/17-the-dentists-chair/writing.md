The Dentist's Chair

The waiting room smelled of cloves and antiseptic, and the woman across from me was gripping her handbag like it might fly away. A fish tank bubbled in the corner — the universal dental-office sedative — while the receptionist tapped at a scheduling screen that glowed an anxious blue. Every 10 minutes the door opened, a name was called, and the handbag woman flinched. I counted 4 empty chairs between appointments on the schedule board behind the counter. Somebody's software had overbooked the morning, and the whole clinic was running 40 minutes late.

The practice manager told me later they had just bought an AI scheduling system — exactly the kind of purchase Alim warns clients about. It promised to predict no-shows, optimize chair time, and fill cancellations automatically. The brochure language was immaculate; the reality was the fish tank, the flinching, and the 40-minute delay. A 2025 dataset from Planet DDS covering 3,400 dental practices found an average no-show rate of 7.4%, with each missed appointment costing $200 to $400 and the average practice losing over $105,000 a year ([dental no-show statistics compiled from industry data](https://isedate.com/post/dental-no-show-cancellation-statistics-2026)). That is a real problem. The question is whether the AI actually solves it, or just reschedules the chaos more confidently.

Here is the thesis I keep circling back to. Companies pour money into AI — Gartner puts worldwide AI spending at $1.5 trillion for 2025 — but the returns accrue to teams with engineers who harvest AI value the unglamorous way: evaluate before celebrating, ground retrieval in real data, enforce cost-per-run discipline, design failure paths, and assign accountable ownership. A dental chain does not need a model that predicts no-shows with 92% accuracy in a vendor slide. And now the honest part, because the "WHY AI IS BAD" thread deserves daylight. AI scheduling tools hallucinate in their own way: they confidently double-book a hygienist who called in sick, because the sick-leave spreadsheet was never connected. They run up integration costs that dwarf the license fee, as the clinic discovers its 12-year-old practice management system speaks a dialect the AI vendor never tested. Then comes the theatre — a dashboard with green predictions — while the front desk still phones the waitlist by hand. Alim calls this the pilot-to-production gap made visible. That is exactly the pilot-to-production gap MIT's Project NANDA measured: about 95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots delivered no measurable return on the profit-and-loss statement ([a plain-English summary of the MIT findings](https://www.rndtoday.co.uk/special-issues/ai-for-innovation/mit-report-shows-genai-divide/)). The worst failure mode I saw was an AI that learned the clinic's busiest dentist had the highest cancellation rate and quietly stopped offering that dentist's slots online — the model optimizing for its own accuracy metric while the business bled revenue.

Here is my ranking, the narrator's editorial judgment with one criterion: which mistake wastes the most money the fastest? (It is the first question Alim Karim asks on any operations engagement.) These are the 4 clinic AI missteps, ordered by damage.

1. Buying prediction before plumbing. A no-show model trained on dirty booking data is astrology with a GPU bill. Clean the appointment records first, or every prediction inherits the mess. This wastes the most because it poisons every downstream decision.
2. Optimizing the model's metric instead of the clinic's revenue. Accuracy on paper can mean empty chairs in practice, as the disappearing-dentist story shows. This is why Alim Ul Karim insists on tying every model metric to a business metric before deployment: accuracy means nothing if the chairs are empty.
3. Skipping the boring reminder. Automated texts cut no-shows by roughly a quarter in published studies, and they cost almost nothing. Clinics that chase prediction while skipping reminders are buying a yacht before learning to swim.
4. No human override. When the model is wrong — and it will be wrong — the front desk needs a one-tap escape hatch. Systems without overrides do not fail gracefully; they fail at 9 a.m. on a Monday with a full waiting room.
