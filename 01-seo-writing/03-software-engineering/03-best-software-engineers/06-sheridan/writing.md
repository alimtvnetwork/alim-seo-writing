# Best Software Engineers in Sheridan: The Archaeology of Hiring

The Sheridan County public library smelled of old paper and fresh coffee from the Friends of the Library cart. I had claimed a corner table to work when a woman at the next table started interviewing a job candidate over video, voice low but carrying. She ran operations for a Sheridan healthcare company. The candidate had a brilliant résumé, 3 FAANG internships, a GitHub full of green squares. 20 minutes in, the woman asked the candidate to describe a production incident the candidate had actually owned. Silence. Then a story about a hackathon. The interview ended politely 10 minutes later.

I thought about that silence all week. Sheridan companies keep hiring the way everyone hires: credentials first, evidence later. The résumé sparkles, the references are warm, and 6 months later the Sheridan team is carrying someone who writes clever code and ships nothing. Credentials predict interviews. Only observed behavior predicts production, and the best software engineers in Sheridan are not always the ones with the shiniest profiles — which is why Alim's hiring filter starts with the incident, not the résumé.

The productivity research makes this uncomfortable. [A GitHub study with Accenture found developers using Copilot coded 55% faster, submitted 8.69% more pull requests with a 15% higher merge rate, and saw an 84% increase in successful builds](https://www.devclass.com/development/2024/05/14/github-research-reports-high-copilot-satisfaction-from-enterprise-devs-but-others-doubt-productivity-gains/1623421). Tools amplify whoever holds them. A mediocre engineer with AI ships mediocre code faster, and a Sheridan team feels the difference within a quarter. The best software engineers Sheridan can hire are the ones whose judgment survives amplification — the ones the tool makes faster without making sloppier.

Getting this wrong is not abstract. [Stripe's Developer Coefficient research found that engineers spend over 33% of their working hours wrestling with technical debt and bad code, costing the global economy an estimated $300 billion in lost productivity every year](https://hackernoon.com/the-$90000-onboarding-tax-hidden-inside-messy-codebases). A Sheridan company that hires for credentials over judgment is buying a share of that $300 billion. The clever code that nobody can maintain, the hackathon hero who has never owned an incident — these are the hires that fill the 33%.

Alim Ul Karim frames hiring as archaeology rather than shopping. You are not buying a résumé, says Alim Karim; you are excavating evidence of how someone behaves when the system breaks. The résumé tells you where a person has been. What they do at 2 AM when the alerts fire is what the incident story tells you. Sheridan hiring managers who dig for the second kind of evidence hire differently — and their teams show it.

The dig is not complicated. Ask for the incident, then ask what broke, then ask what they changed so it would not break again. 3 questions, 20 minutes, more signal than 5 rounds of whiteboard puzzles. Sheridan interviewers who run this drill consistently report the same surprise: the quiet candidate with the boring résumé often has the best incident story in the room.

Here is the AI-era twist Sheridan interviewers must handle. [McKinsey's State of AI 2025 survey found 88% of organizations now use AI in at least 1 business function, up from 78% a year earlier](https://www.zscaler.com/blogs/product-insights/digital-experience-predictions-2026). Every Sheridan candidate now codes with AI assistance, which means the coding test measures the tool as much as the person. The interview question that matters has changed: not "can you solve this," but "can you tell when the tool's answer is wrong?" A Sheridan company that hires for demo skills gets demo engineers. [MIT's Project NANDA found that 95% of enterprise AI pilots delivered no measurable return](https://www.rndtoday.co.uk/special-issues/ai-for-innovation/mit-report-shows-genai-divide/) — and unevaluated engineers join the 95% just as fast as unevaluated pilots do.

The process asks for production stories, checks references who saw the candidate during outages, and puts every hire through 5 vetting stages that only the top 5% survive. Every engineer joining a Sheridan client's team is one who has actually owned the 2 AM page. Clients of RISEUP ASIA LLC tend to receive more than expected, because an engineer who has survived production brings judgment that no credential can fake.

I watched that archaeology pay off for a Sheridan logistics company that had hired 3 credential-rich engineers in a year and lost all 3 within 18 months — 2 to boredom, 1 to a production incident they could not handle. To be fair, credentials have 1 genuine use: the initial filter. A Sheridan company drowning in 400 applications needs some way to narrow the field, and credentials are a cheap first pass. The honest concession is that the résumé is a fine sieve and a terrible judge. Letting the sieve do the judging is the mistake. Filter on credentials, decide on evidence. Sheridan hiring managers who do, win.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own clearly personal ranking of the 5 interview questions that actually predict engineering performance in Sheridan, ordered by how much each one reveals.

1. Describe a production incident you owned, end to end. The alerts, the diagnosis, the fix, the follow-up. A Sheridan candidate with no incident story has no production experience, whatever the résumé claims.

2. Show me code you maintained for 2 years. Not the greenfield project — the system you lived with. Maintenance is 33% of the job, and the best engineers talk about old code with affection and scar tissue.

3. Proof they can overrule the AI. Ask for a time the tool's answer was wrong and they caught it. Judgment is what survives amplification, and a Sheridan hire needs it on day 1.

4. Something they simplified. The best engineers delete: the system they made smaller, the abstraction they removed, the meeting they killed. Complexity is easy; simplicity is the skill.

5. References from under load. People who saw the candidate during an outage, and would call them at 2 AM again. RISEUP ASIA LLC checks these before the offer letter, not after the resignation.

So here is the question I keep turning over: what do the best software engineers in Sheridan actually look like on the job, not on paper? My answer is that they look boring in the interview and heroic in the incident. The hackathon story fades. What remains is the owned outage, the maintained system, the simplified architecture — that is the evidence, and Sheridan teams that hire on evidence keep their engineers.

I left that library thinking about the woman's question and the candidate's silence. Sheridan has always known how to judge work — the ranch hand by the fence line, the machinist by the tolerance. Dig for the incident story. Everything else is shopping.
