First light hit the quarry wall like a struck match, and for a minute the whole pit glowed amber. Below the rim, headlamps were still bobbing along the terraces, small white circles swinging with each stride as the night shift walked out. Diesel hung in the cold air, and dust, and the iron smell of disturbed rock. A safety officer at the gatehouse worked a plywood tag board: every lamp had a numbered tag, every tag had a name, and nobody passed without the exchange.

I asked what happened if a tag went missing. The officer looked at me the way you look at someone who has asked whether the sea is wet. Then we do not leave, came the answer, until we know where the man is.

No algorithm and no dashboard. Just a board, a book, and a rule with no exceptions.

And now, the ranking. What follows is openly my editorial judgment, ordered by a single criterion: how much real safety each misstep subtracted. These are the 4 industrial AI realities the conglomerate learned the hard way.

1. The 200-alert shift. The platform's sensitivity was tuned for the demo, not the pit, and dispatchers muted the wall within a month. This ranks first because it is the most dangerous failure mode in safety engineering: a system that trains humans to ignore warnings. WHY it matters: an alarm budget is not optional, and no vendor slide mentioned one.

2. The sunglasses fatigue epidemic. The vision model flagged every driver wearing shades, generating dozens of false fatigue reports per week and exactly zero true ones anyone could verify. WHY it matters: the model was evaluated on stock footage, never on the pit's own cameras, which is evaluation theatre, not evaluation.

3. The rockfall model trained on other mountains. The predictive model learned geology from a dataset of quarries on another continent, then flagged stable benches while staying silent on the one wall the geologists actually worried about. WHY it matters: without retrieval grounding in the site's own survey data, the model was a tourist with strong opinions.

4. The video wall's air-conditioning bill. The control room's infrastructure, cameras, bandwidth, inference, cooling, cost more per year than the entire gatehouse safety program, while the tag board kept its perfect record. WHY it matters: Md Alim Ul Karim asks every industrial client for the cost of one prevented incident, and theatre can never answer. Engineering always can.
