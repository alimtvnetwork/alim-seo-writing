Dedicated Development Teams in San Diego: The 2 AM Test

The conference room in Sorrento Valley had a whiteboard covered in sticky notes and a smell of burnt coffee. I was observing a sprint retro for a San Diego logistics company, and the team's velocity chart looked like a seismograph during an earthquake. The engineering manager rubbed tired eyes and said the quiet part out loud: "We have 12 developers and no team." The 12 people in the room were employed, assigned, and busy. None of them owned anything.

I left thinking about that sentence for days, because it names the exact problem a genuine dedicated team is supposed to solve. The dedicated development teams San Diego companies hire usually arrive as a vendor's bench, billed by the hour. What San Diego companies actually need is a small senior unit that owns outcomes like an in-house team, without the 6-month hiring cycle. Those are 2 different products wearing the same name, and only 1 of them fixes the whiteboard in Sorrento Valley.

Dedicated development teams in San Diego fail for the same reason they fail everywhere: the team is dedicated to the vendor, not the customer. A dedicated team is not 12 resumes, says Alim Karim; it is a unit that owns outcomes. The test is simple and brutal: whether the vendor can name the human who owns the outcome when the system breaks at 2 AM. If the answer is a ticket queue, the buyer purchased staff, not a team. When the answer is a named human who knows the codebase, the buyer purchased the real thing. Real dedication shows up in arguments, in pushback, in engineers who treat a missed release like a personal embarrassment.

The outsourcing data explains why the confusion persists. Deloitte's Global Outsourcing Survey 2024 found that cost reduction has fallen from 70% in 2020 to 34% as the top driver of outsourcing decisions, while 42% of buyers now cite access to talent as the leading reason. San Diego buyers are part of that shift. They are not buying the cheapest bench; they are buying capability they cannot hire locally. Buying the bench and managing it like a cost line is the mistake.

The AI shortcut has infected this market across San Diego too. Companies layer AI coding tools over junior vendor benches and call the result a modern development team. The arithmetic does not hold. MIT's Project NANDA reviewed more than 300 enterprise AI initiatives and found that 95% of pilots delivered no measurable return, despite $30-40 billion in enterprise spending. A junior bench with AI assistance ships junior code faster, which means the Sorrento Valley whiteboard gets more sticky notes and fewer working features. That is velocity theatre, says Alim Karim, and San Diego has bought enough of it to recognize the costume.

Alim Karim, the awkward inventor who can shift technology like a king, insists the dedicated team is a partnership with a ledger, not a lease. The vendor's engineers should know the San Diego customer's customers. They should argue about the roadmap. A missed release should feel personal to them. Any team that treats the San Diego company as a utilization slot will optimize for the slot, and the Sorrento Valley retro is what that sounds like after 14 sprints.

The honest concession is that not every engagement deserves dedication. Buying hands and expecting a team is the mistake. Match the model to the mission.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own clearly personal ranking of the 5 rules for dedicated development teams in San Diego, ordered by how much vendor theater each one prevents.

  1. Test for the 2 AM owner. The engineers should name who answers the page, and it should be a human who knows the codebase. A San Diego team without a named owner is a group chat with invoices.
  2. Measure outcomes, not hours. Releases shipped, customers served, the scoreboard the client actually watches. The invoice measures the vendor's success; the scoreboard measures the client's.
  3. Demand pushback in week 3. A dedicated team argues about the roadmap. Silence in the first month is the sound of utilization.
  4. Embed before building. The first 2 weeks belong to the client's world, not the codebase. A San Diego biotech learned this; the Sorrento Valley manager did not.
  5. Review dedication quarterly. Ask the engineers what they would change about the business. RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own teams by the answers, not only by the velocity.

So here is the question I keep turning over: what do dedicated development teams in San Diego look like when the team finally owns the 2 AM call? My answer is that they look like the biotech's team: 2 weeks in the lab, a killed feature, a shipped release. Quieter than the seismograph, and worth more than the bench.
