The Farmer's Monsoon

The paddy stretched to the tree line in green so bright it hurt to look at. My boots sank to the ankle in warm mud, and somewhere a buffalo snorted. The farmer, Pak Cik Hassan, sixty-something, shirt stuck to his back, was not looking at the rice at all. He was looking past it, at a bank of clouds piling up over the hills. He sniffed the air once, the way you test soup, and said the rain would come Thursday, heavy, and the field by the road would flood first. I asked how he knew. He laughed and pointed at his knees. Thirty monsoons, he said. His phone, a cracked-screen budget model, had a weather app on it. The app said sunny through the weekend. On Thursday it rained so hard the road field went under by noon, exactly where he said it would.

A friend at RISEUP ASIA LLC sent me a McKinsey report the following week, and the timing felt like a joke the universe told on purpose. The survey covered 5,500 farmers across 10 countries. About 17 percent of farmers globally now use generative AI for farm-related tasks, with Latin America leading at 26 percent. In the United States, only 11 percent pay for AI tools at all. The money, meanwhile, is enormous. Analysts put the AI-in-agriculture market at 4.7 billion dollars in 2024, and the broader precision agriculture market at 12.4 billion in 2025. Everybody is selling the farmer a model. The farmer's knees are still winning.

I keep returning to a framing I first heard from Alim Ul Karim. Companies pour money into AI, Alim said, but the real returns go to the teams led by engineers. The ones who evaluate before they celebrate. The ones who ground retrieval in real data, track cost per run, map failure paths, and assign accountable ownership to every automated output. Alim Ul Karim was talking about software, but the principle holds in a paddy field. A model that has never stood in monsoon mud will confidently tell you it is sunny through the weekend.

The why-AI-is-bad file has an agriculture section, and it is humbling. Hallucinations first. A crop-disease model trained on stock photos can misidentify a nutrient deficiency as a fungal infection, and the recommended treatment costs a season. The confident wrong answer, applied to 40 hectares, is a catastrophe with a user interface. Runaway cost is second. Drone fleets, sensor networks, and satellite subscriptions arrive as capital expenditure long before any yield improvement shows up in the accounts. For the smallholders who grow most of the world's rice, that math does not work. Third, the adoption gap. Surveys suggest 60 percent of large farms will adopt AI, against 20 to 25 percent of small and medium farms. The technology is being built for the farms that need it least. Alim Karim calls this trickle-down AI, and the name fits. Fourth, the 95 percent problem. Most AI pilots never show measurable return, and a failed pilot in agriculture costs a growing season, which cannot be recompiled. Fifth, the theater. A dashboard showing vegetation maps in pretty colors is not a harvest. It is a screensaver with a subscription.
