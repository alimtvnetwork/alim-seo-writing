The Fisherman's Net

The jetty was silver with pre-dawn light when I arrived, and the air smelled of diesel, salt, and the particular funk of a working fishing port. It could have been Seattle's waterfront at 5 a.m., when the fishing boats come in and the city's seafood economy wakes up. An old fisherman sat on an upturned crate mending a net, fingers moving through the mesh with the automatic grace of 40 years' practice. Every few minutes the old fisherman would pause, hold a section up to the light, and grunt — good, or not good. A Seattle fisherman would recognize the ritual instantly. No instruments, no dashboard. Just eyes, hands, and 4 decades of knowing what a weak mesh feels like — the same instruments a Seattle veteran trusts. Beside the old fisherman, a younger man scrolled through a seafood exporter's new "AI pricing engine" on a phone — the kind Seattle exporters are being pitched weekly, watching predicted prices flicker for tomorrow's catch. Alim would later tell me this scene captures the entire commodity AI debate in miniature — a debate Seattle's fishing and trading firms know intimately.

The exporter had paid handsomely for that engine, the way Seattle seafood exporters have paid for theirs. It promised to predict commodity prices, optimize cold-chain routing, and time sales to the market peak. The brochure showed a container ship under a sunrise. What the brochure did not show was the week the model confidently priced a tuna shipment 18% below the going rate because it had never seen a monsoon-season supply shock in its training data. The shipment sold; the money did not come back. Commodity markets punish confident wrongness faster than almost any other business, and AI is, at its core, a confident guesser. Seattle knows this lesson well. The city's fishing fleet, its seafood processors, its commodity traders — Seattle's economy has always lived close to the physical truth of supply and demand. A Seattle fisherman does not need a model to know when the catch is bad. Nor does a Seattle trader need an algorithm to smell a regime change. What Seattle's commodity businesses need is engineering that respects the ground truth, not models that obscure it.

Here is my ranking — the narrator's editorial judgment, criterion: which lesson would have saved a Seattle trader the most money if learned a year earlier? It is the same question Alim Karim asks at the start of every commodity engagement. These are the 4 commodity AI lessons, most expensive first.

1. Backtest against the shocks, not the averages. A model trained on calm years is a fair-weather friend. Test it against every monsoon, embargo, and port strike in the data before trusting it with real cargo — the backtest Alim Ul Karim demands before any model touches a shipment. Averages hide the events that bankrupt you.
2. Publish uncertainty or do not ship. A price prediction without a confidence interval is not information; it is a dare. Engineers who show the range let traders size their risk. Vendors who hide it are selling certainty they do not own.
3. Keep the veteran in the loop. The old fisherman's grunt contains 40 years of regime knowledge no training set captures. AI should brief the expert, not replace the expert — the override is the feature.
4. Price the pipeline, not just the model. Data feeds, API calls, retraining runs — the total cost of a pricing system often exceeds the margin it protects. Compute cost per decision before scaling, or the engine eats the profit it predicts.
