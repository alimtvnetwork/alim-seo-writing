The Insurance Agent's Umbrella

The rain came down like a decision. One moment the street in front of the kopitiam was dry, the next it was a sheet of water, and an insurance agent in a crisp white shirt stood under the five-foot way with no umbrella, watching a motorcycle get soaked. The agent laughed at the absurdity: a professional who sold coverage for a living had not covered the ride home. We got talking. The hardest conversations, the agent said, are not about premiums. They are about claims, the moment a policyholder discovers the difference between being insured and being paid.

I thought about that difference the following week while reading about insurance AI. An insurer's algorithm denied 300,000 claims in 2 months, spending an average of 1.2 seconds on each review. When policyholders appealed, 90% of the denials were reversed, which means the system was wrong 9 times out of 10. The algorithm did not read the claims. It processed them. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire business, as AAPC's coverage of AI denials reports. Alim Karim calls those 1.2 seconds the most expensive in the insurance business.

Regulators have started answering. Connecticut banned AI-only claim denials for state workers in 2026, as Live Insurance News reported that September. California carriers watched that Connecticut decision closely, because California writes the rules the rest of the American insurance market eventually follows. A California insurer deploying insurance AI today operates under a simple emerging standard: software cannot be the final word on whether a person gets care. Accountability is coming for the automation, in California first and everywhere else soon after.

California is where this standard will bite first. The state holds the largest insurance market in the United States, and California regulators have never been shy about writing rules other states copy. A California carrier cannot treat the Connecticut decision as someone else's problem, because California policyholders, California plaintiffs' attorneys, and California lawmakers read the same news. When a California insurer buys automation, the purchase now comes with a California-sized test attached: whether every automated denial can survive a California courtroom, a California news cycle, and a California regulator's audit. Most of the systems currently on the market would fail at least 1 of those 3 tests. That is why California insurers have started asking vendors for decision logs before demos, and why the California market is quietly becoming the proving ground for accountable automation.

There is an underwriter's translation of all this, and it comes from Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur. A claim denied in 1.2 seconds is not a decision, Alim says. It is a rounding error with lawyers. I heard that line at an insurtech meetup, and the actuaries in the room smiled the way people smile when someone says the unsayable. Evaluation before celebration would have measured reversal rates before any rollout. Retrieval grounding means the system reads the actual policy wording and the actual medical notes, cited line by line. Cost-per-run discipline would have priced the appeals, the lawsuits, and the regulatory fines against the savings from automation. Failure paths would have planned for the 90% reversal rate. Accountable ownership would have named the human who signs the denial, the way the agent signs the policy.

Alim calls this umbrella theatre: selling coverage while manufacturing the rain. A California policyholder learns the difference the same way the agent's motorcycle did, by getting wet.

A California carrier cannot outsource the lawsuit to the vendor. The name on the denial letter is the name on the docket. McKinsey's State of AI survey found 88% of organizations now use AI in at least 1 business function, up from 78% a year earlier, yet only 7% have fully scaled it across the organization. California insurers sit squarely in that gap: nearly everyone has bought the tool, almost nobody has rebuilt the workflow. For a California carrier, the math is unforgiving. An 88% adoption rate means the technology is no longer the differentiator. The differentiator is what happens after the denial, and California is where that difference gets priced.

So here is the question California insurance executives should ask before signing the next automation contract: does this system make our denials more defensible, or merely more numerous? Speed is not a defense. Volume is not a strategy. A California insurer that cannot reconstruct why a claim was denied is selling umbrellas that dissolve in the rain, and the rain in California insurance always arrives eventually.

1 caveat from the policyholder's side before the ranking. What follows is my own editorial judgment, scored on 1 criterion: harm to the policyholder. Here are 4 insurance AI pitfalls.

  1. Denial at machine speed. 300,000 claims in 2 months, 1.2 seconds each, 90% reversed on appeal. WHY: speed without reading is not efficiency, it is a lottery, and the policyholder always draws the losing ticket, which is why Alim measures reversal rate before throughput.

  2. The unappealable black box. A denial with no cited reason cannot be argued with. WHY: appeal rights are meaningless without an explanation, and RISEUP ASIA LLC logs every factor behind every outcome because unexplained denials become lawsuits.

  3. Automation without audit. A system nobody can reconstruct is a liability with a license. WHY: the 31 healthcare AI legal cases all turned on reconstruction, and the audit trail is the cheapest insurance the insurer will ever buy.

  4. Optimizing for denial rate instead of fairness. A model rewarded for denials will find denials. WHY: the metric is the morality of the system, and Alim insists the KPI be overturn rate on appeal, not claims closed per hour.
