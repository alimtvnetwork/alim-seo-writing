Legacy Modernization in Wyoming: Do Not Turn Off

The server closet in the back of the Wyoming utility office hummed the way old refrigerators hum, steady and slightly worried. A beige tower with a floppy drive sat beside a rack of blinking switches, and a handwritten label on the tower read DO NOT TURN OFF, underlined 3 times. The office manager told me the billing system on that machine was older than everyone in the building except the manager, and the only person who fully understood it had retired to Arizona 2 years ago.

That closet exists in hundreds of Wyoming organizations. Banks in Casper, county offices in Laramie, energy firms in Gillette, and the whole spread of Wyoming industry between them all run on systems that work, barely, and that nobody dares to touch. Then a vendor arrives with a slide deck about digital transformation, the board approves a modernization budget, and the project begins with a big-bang rewrite. 18 months later the beige tower is still humming, the new system is half-built, and the Wyoming organization is paying for both.

Legacy modernization in Wyoming is the state's least glamorous technology problem and its most expensive one. Most Wyoming legacy systems are oracles, not ruins. Treat them accordingly.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own clearly personal ranking of the 5 rules for legacy modernization in Wyoming, ordered by how much disaster each one prevents.

  1. Document behavior before changing anything. What the system does, drawn from real transactions, not manuals. A Wyoming team that cannot describe the current behavior cannot specify the replacement.
  2. Build the oracle harness. Thousands of test cases from observed reality, run against every change. The 95% failure pattern is mostly the price of changing what you never measured.
  3. Strangle, do not detonate. Replace 1 module at a time while the old system keeps working. The cutover nobody notices is the successful one.
  4. Keep the business rules in human language. Every rule the old system enforced, written down where a Wyoming auditor can read it. Context evaporates; documentation is the backup.
  5. Never let AI rewrite unattended. Use it for tests, boilerplate, and drafts — with human review on every line. RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own modernization work by what the AI was not allowed to touch.
