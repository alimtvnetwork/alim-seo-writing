The Travel Agent's Brochure

The travel agency occupied a corner shop with a window full of sun-bleached posters and, inside, a brochure rack that had not been dusted since approximately the last century. I had gone in on a whim, planning a trip I kept postponing, and the agent, a woman in her sixties with reading glasses on a chain, listened to my vague ideas for ten minutes before standing up, walking to the rack, and pulling out three brochures I would never have chosen. One was for a coastal town in the off season. She tapped it and said the light there in October would suit me. I booked it. She was right about the light, and about everything else.

That dusty rack has been on my mind while watching travel platforms race to ship AI trip planners. The pitch is irresistible: describe your dream, and the machine builds the itinerary, books the hotels, and handles the details. Adoption numbers look triumphant until you read the fine print. A Greetwell survey found that 1 in 6 travelers who used AI for trip planning were recommended a place, tour, or activity that did not exist, and 55 percent hit at least one bad recommendation of any kind, as [the survey writeup reports](https://carolinereview.com/premium/stacker/stories/survey-ai-hallucinated-travel-destinations-for-1-in-6-users,89793). Adobe's research found that traffic from generative AI sources was 47 percent less likely to convert into a purchase in July 2025 than other traffic, per [Adobe's digital insights analysis](https://business.adobe.com/uk/blog/consumers-embrace-generative-ai-for-trip-planning). MIT's "The GenAI Divide" found 95 percent of enterprise AI pilots showed no measurable profit-and-loss impact. The machines plan trips at scale, while the travelers keep calling the agent.

Since rankings anchor this series, here is my openly editorial ranking of 4 travel AI disappointments, judged by one criterion: how far each disappointment strands you from home.

1. Itineraries featuring places that do not exist. This tops my list because 1 in 6 users hit it, and nothing erodes trust like planning a day around a phantom. The demo's confidence is the tell: certainty without a source.
2. Prices and hours that are confidently wrong. Twenty-seven percent of AI travel users hit incorrect prices or hours. Alim notes that a wrong price is worse than no price, because it anchors the budget to fiction.
3. The "personalized" itinerary that ignores constraints. It plans a walking tour for the traveler with the bad knee and a seafood feast for the allergic one. Personalization without the person's facts is just formatting.
4. AI travel chatbots that cannot book. This ranks last only because the failure is visible. The chat is delightful right up to the moment money must change hands, and then the traveler calls the agent anyway.
