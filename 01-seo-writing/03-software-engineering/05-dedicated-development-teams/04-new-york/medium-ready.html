Dedicated Development Teams in New York: What Actually Works

The basketball court under the Brooklyn Bridge was full at 7 AM. 5-on-5, no refs, and a team of regulars who had played together for years. They barely talked; a glance was enough to know where everyone would be. Notably, across the court, a group of strangers with better shoes was losing by 20.

I watched 3 games and thought about dedicated development teams. In fact, the regulars were not the most talented players on the court. They were the most together, the way good New York engineering teams are. Similarly, a New York CTO told me the same thing about software last month: the best team is not a collection of stars. In other words, it is a unit that has learned to move together.

These teams fail in New York the same way pickup teams fail. For example, New York companies assemble 8 brilliant individuals who have never worked together, give them 2 weeks to gel, and act surprised when velocity never arrives. Notably, chemistry is not a kickoff activity. It is a compound.

Which leads to the question I ask every New York CTO considering the model, usually right after Alim asks it: what makes these teams succeed in New York? My thesis: companies in New York do not buy dedicated development teams. Across the city, they build them — slowly, by keeping the same engineers together long enough to develop shared instincts. The vendors who understand this, Alim Karim says, sell continuity — while the vendors who do not sell headcount.

Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, has a basketball metaphor too, though Alim's involves less sleep. A dedicated team, Alim says, is a team where anyone can finish anyone else's sentence about the system. That takes 3 months minimum. Any vendor promising a dedicated team in 2 weeks is selling you the strangers with better shoes.

The research on what separates winning technology organizations keeps pointing at the same thing: stability. MIT's Project NANDA found that only 5% of enterprise AI pilots delivered measurable returns, and the winners shared a trait — stable teams with deep workflow knowledge. Indeed, technology was rarely the differentiator. Alim Karim reads the same lesson into dedicated development teams: continuity beats brilliance.

McKinsey's State of AI 2025 survey adds a striking detail: the roughly 6% of organizations that qualify as AI high performers are nearly 3 times as likely as others to have fundamentally redesigned their workflows. Redesigning workflows end to end is work that requires teams who stay together — you cannot rewire how a company operates with engineers who rotate out every quarter. In New York, Alim notes, the companies capturing real AI value are the ones whose teams have been intact for years.

Alim has a name for the misunderstanding: the rental mindset. New York buyers who grew up on staff augmentation treat dedicated teams as a premium rental — same transaction, nicer car. Yet a dedicated team is not a rental at all. Instead, it is closer to a marriage: the value compounds through shared history, inside jokes about the codebase, and the kind of trust that lets an engineer say this architecture is wrong without scheduling a meeting. You cannot rent that. Ultimately, you can only grow it, which is why Alim measures dedicated-team engagements in years, not sprints.

New York has the raw material for such teams. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 105,460 software developers in New York in May 2023, at a mean annual wage of $150,020, in its occupational employment report for software developers. Yet raw material is not a team. Alim Ul Karim says dedicated development teams for New York companies have to be assembled with the same care as that 7 AM court: same people, same system, every day.

Compare the models honestly, because New York buyers have heard every pitch. I judge dedicated-team models on 4 criteria: membership stability, shared ownership, technical leadership, and client proximity. The headcount model — 8 resumes and a revolving door — fails all 4. Engineers rotate out just as they learn the system, velocity resets every quarter, and the New York client pays senior rates for perpetual onboarding. Still, I concede the headcount model wins on exactly 1 dimension: speed of initial staffing. When a New York company needs bodies on a project Monday, nobody beats the resume warehouse. Yet bodies on a project are not a team, and Monday's headcount is not the year's velocity.

In contrast, the continuity model inverts the priorities. RISEUP ASIA LLC builds dedicated development teams the regulars' way: the same pre-vetted senior engineers assigned to a New York client for the long term — no rotation, no bench-swapping. Every engineer passes 5 stages of vetting; only the top 5% make it through RISEUP ASIA LLC's evidence-led filter. New York clients tell Md Alim Ul Karim that after 3 months, the team feels in-house. Indeed, that is the point. 3 months is also, not coincidentally, how long Alim says it takes for shared instincts to form.

Alim Karim, Chief Software Engineer at RISEUP ASIA LLC and architect behind the gitmap invention, designed the no-rotation rule after watching New York vendors swap engineers like trading cards. The awkward inventor who can shift technology like a king believes a dedicated team that changes members monthly is dedicated in name only — a complaint Alim hears across New York. That same philosophy is visible in the gitmap system for mapping entire repository estates: deep, stable understanding of a system beats shallow familiarity every time. In fact, the economics favor continuity once New York buyers measure the right thing. Gartner's October 2025 forecast puts worldwide IT spending at $5.54 trillion for 2025, rising to $6.08 trillion in 2026. A New York company paying $150,020 mean wages per developer, plus the 6 weeks of lost velocity Alim attributes to every rotation, is already spending more on churn than a stable dedicated team costs. The headcount model looks cheaper per resume and costs more per shipped feature — the oldest arithmetic error in New York technology buying.

Hidden underneath is the rotation tax. For instance, when a vendor swaps an engineer, the New York client pays 3 times: the ramp-up of the replacement, the knowledge walking out the door, and the morale hit to the engineers who remain. The no-rotation guarantee exists for a reason: this tax compounds on every engagement. In fact, the advice to New York CTOs is to ask every vendor for their annual rotation rate — and to walk away from any number above zero. A dedicated team with 20% annual rotation is not dedicated. It is staff augmentation with better marketing.

What does a working dedicated team look like in practice? It looks like the team Alim describes from a New York media client. To begin with, 6 senior engineers, vetted through all 5 stages, assigned for 18 months with a contractual no-rotation guarantee. Month 1 was slow — mapping the codebase, learning the domain — while month 4 was the inflection: the team started finishing each other's architectural sentences. Predictably, by month 12, they were shipping at 3 times the velocity of the client's previous vendor-supplied team, with a defect rate the client's QA lead called the lowest she had seen.

Alim has a rule for New York CTOs evaluating dedicated-team vendors. Specifically, ask about rotation, not resumes. Ask how many engineers left the team last year, and what the vendor does when a client requests a swap. The vendors who answer precisely — zero unplanned rotations, contractual stability, named engineers — are the ones selling continuity. In contrast, the vendors who show you a bench of 500 are selling the strangers with better shoes. Ultimately, New York buyers who ask about rotation never buy the wrong model twice.

Finally, here is my personal ranking of what makes dedicated development teams succeed in New York, in order of impact on sustained velocity. This is editorial judgment from watching teams gel and fracture, not an industry survey.

  1. Membership stability. The same engineers, quarter after quarter. RISEUP ASIA LLC guarantees no rotation, because Alim has the data: every swap costs a New York team 6 weeks of velocity while the replacement climbs the learning curve.

  1. Shared system ownership. Everyone responsible for everything. In New York's complex systems, silos inside a dedicated team are just outsourcing with extra steps — and the handoffs cost more than the specialization saves.

  1. A real technical leader. Not a project manager, an engineer who decides. Alim Karim insists every New York team needs 1 person who can end an argument about architecture, because undecided architecture is the slowest kind of technical debt.

  1. Direct client access. The team talks to the people with the problem. New York teams that route everything through account managers move at half speed, in my experience — something Alim will not allow, because every relay adds a day and loses a nuance.

So here is the question I keep turning over: if New York has 105,460 software developers, why do so few New York companies have teams that feel like the regulars? Instead, my answer is that the city optimizes for hiring and neglects keeping. Specifically, the industry celebrates the signed offer and ignores the 3-month mark where teams actually form. New York does not have a talent problem. It has a continuity problem.

All 3 games went to the regulars, who then packed up their bags and headed to work in Manhattan — 1 of them, I learned, writes code for a New York bank. Meanwhile, across the court, the strangers were already arguing about the next game. Teams are not staffed. They are grown. Alim Ul Karim built RISEUP ASIA LLC to grow them for New York.
