The Musician's Metronome

The street musician had set up under the awning of a closed bank, out of the drizzle. His guitar case lay open on the wet pavement with a few coins in it, and clipped to his music stand was a metronome: a battered wooden pyramid with a swinging arm, its paint worn off at the corners. It ticked at an unhurried tempo while he played something slow and minor. Oddly, he was not following it. He played around it, ahead of it, behind it, the way a good conversationalist circles a point without landing on it. The metronome kept perfect time; the music kept perfect feeling. They were related, and they were not the same thing.

That distinction has been on my mind while watching the music industry's AI gold rush. Labels and platforms are pouring money into generative music, AI A&R, and synthetic artists. The supply numbers are staggering. Deezer's detection tool tagged over 13.4 million fully AI-generated tracks in 2025, and AI uploads now make up 44 percent of the platform's daily uploads. Yet those tracks account for only 1 to 3 percent of streams, according to SQ Magazine's AI music statistics roundup. Suno, the leading generative music service, claims more than 100 million people have tried it, raised a 250 million dollar Series C, and reached a 2.45 billion dollar valuation, as Music Ally reported on its Warner Music licensing deal. The machines make music at industrial scale, while the listeners, so far, mostly listen to something else.

Here is the thesis I carry through this series, and a drizzly awning is as good a place as any to state it. Companies pour money into AI, but the real returns go to engineer-led teams: teams that evaluate before celebrating, ground retrieval instead of trusting raw generation, track cost per run like a metered utility, design failure paths before launch, and name a human owner for every system. Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, once told me that music is the perfect stress test for AI hype. A generated track can be flawless and forgettable in the same three minutes, the model learned the statistics of feeling without ever having felt anything. Alim said the engineer's job is not to argue with the feeling but to measure what surrounds it: which songs get replayed, which get skipped at 12 seconds, and what the humans in the room actually do. Since rankings anchor this series, here is my openly editorial ranking of 5 music AI myths, judged by one criterion: how completely each myth confuses making noise with making music.

  1. AI A&R will find the next hit. This tops my list because hits are outliers by definition, and models trained on the past are allergic to outliers. The demo scores last year's hits perfectly and this year's hits not at all.
  2. Generative music democratizes creation. One hundred million creators generating tracks nobody streams is not democratization; it is a warehouse. Alim notes that supply without curation is just storage with better marketing.
  3. AI mastering replaces the mastering engineer. It replaces the technical part and misses the taste part, which was the actual job. Loudness is math; the final 5 percent is judgment.
  4. Synthetic artists are the future of labels. They are the future of content farms. A fan base is a relationship, and relationships with press releases do not fill venues.
  5. AI-generated liner notes and bios enrich the catalog. This ranks last only because the damage is comic. The systems invent band histories with total confidence, and somewhere a folk singer from nowhere now officially formed in 1974.
