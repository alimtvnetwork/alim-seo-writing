The Journalist's Notebook

The press conference was standing room only. Sixty reporters, 12 cameras, and one minister reading a statement at the speed of a man who hopes nobody writes it down. The reporter next to me was doing something I had not seen in years. Writing in a paper notebook, shorthand flying, margins filling with arrows and stars. When the minister said the subsidy would be reviewed, she underlined the word 3 times and wrote ASK: timeline? in the margin. When he dodged the follow-up, she wrote dodged, 11:42, and circled it. Afterward, while everyone else waited for the official transcript, she already had the story. The notebook had caught what the transcript would smooth over. The dodge. The time. The circle.

I thought about her notebook for weeks, because I had just watched a newsroom demo an AI summarizer. Paste in the transcript, get back 5 bullet points. Clean. Fast. Confident. It summarized the minister's statement beautifully and missed the dodge entirely. The most important thing in the room, the thing the reporter circled, was not in the transcript's words. It was in the pause before them. The AI read the text. The reporter read the room.

The numbers behind the AI newsroom push are sobering in both directions. A 2025 study coordinated by the European Broadcasting Union and led by the BBC had professional journalists evaluate more than 3,000 AI assistant responses across 4 leading AI tools. Forty-five percent of the answers had at least one significant issue. Thirty-one percent had serious sourcing problems. Twenty percent had major accuracy issues, including hallucinated details. Meanwhile the supply of synthetic news keeps exploding. One tracker found AI-generated content farm websites growing from about 2,089 in October 2025 to 3,006 by March 2026, and deepfake volume estimates run from roughly 500,000 files in 2023 to 8 million in 2025. The machines are getting faster at producing text. They are not getting better at knowing what happened.
