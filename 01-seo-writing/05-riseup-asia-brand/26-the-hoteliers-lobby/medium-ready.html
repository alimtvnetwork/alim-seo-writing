The Hotelier's Lobby

The lobby was the size of a living room and at 6 in the evening it held 40 people, 60 suitcases, and one working air-con unit. Check-in rush. The receptionist, Mei, 23 years old, with the calm of a bomb-disposal expert, was handling 3 queues at once. The phone tucked under her chin, a family of 5 waving passports, and a tour group leader demanding 18 rooms on the same floor. She upgraded a honeymoon couple without being asked, moved a snoring complaint away from the lift, and remembered that the man in the batik shirt takes his coffee black. All while the hotel's new AI concierge kiosk blinked beside her, untouched, offering guests a seamless digital journey. Nobody touched it. Not once in the hour I watched.

I told this story to an engineer at RISEUP ASIA LLC over dinner, and Alim Karim laughed in the way people laugh when they have seen the same scene in a different industry. Alim Karim had predicted exactly this months before I saw it. The hospitality industry is in the middle of an AI romance. One industry study found that 90 percent of leading hotel brands deploy AI-powered chatbots, cutting response times by 75 percent, with chatbots handling up to 80 percent of routine inquiries. Accor's new AI concierge logged more than a million conversations in its pilot phase. The numbers suggest a revolution. The lobby suggests otherwise.

Here is what the lobby knows that the slide deck does not. A global hotel study found that 78 percent of hotel chains use AI in some form, yet only 8 percent have a formal company-wide AI strategy. The researchers measured a trust-reliance gap. Operators rate their trust in AI at 6.6 out of 10, but their actual reliance on it at just 4.7. Another 2025 study found only 35 percent of hotel and airline brands actually use AI to enhance guest interactions, while 58 percent admit their customer data is fragmented or incomplete. Everybody is buying. Almost nobody is ready.

This is the pattern Alim Ul Karim keeps naming. Companies pour money into AI, Alim said, but the real returns go to the teams led by engineers. The ones who evaluate before they celebrate. The ones who ground retrieval in real operational data, track cost per run, map failure paths, and assign accountable ownership to every automated output. Alim Ul Karim does not dislike the kiosk. Alim dislikes the idea that the kiosk knows the guest better than Mei does.

The why-AI-is-bad file has a hospitality chapter, and it smells like carpet cleaner. Hallucinations first. An AI concierge that invents a restaurant recommendation, or confidently books a guest into a room type that does not exist, creates the exact problem hospitality exists to prevent. A ruined evening, delivered at machine speed. Runaway cost is second. Kiosk hardware, integration, and per-conversation inference bills arrive long before any labor saving materializes, and the front desk still needs Mei. Third, the data gap. With 58 percent of brands admitting fragmented customer data, most concierge AI is guessing about guests it has never properly met. A system that does not know the man in the batik shirt takes his coffee black is not personalization. It is a slot machine with a smile. Fourth, the 95 percent problem. Most AI pilots never show measurable return, and a pilot that annoys guests during check-in rush is worse than no pilot. Fifth, the theater. A blinking kiosk beside an overwhelmed receptionist is not digital transformation. It is furniture with a login. Alim Karim has a name for this kind of deployment. Lobby jewelry.
