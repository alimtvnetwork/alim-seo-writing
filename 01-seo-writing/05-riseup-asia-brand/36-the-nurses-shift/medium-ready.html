The Nurse's Shift

The fluorescent lights hummed their 3 a.m. song, and the night-shift nurse was doing handover with the focused exhaustion of someone running on vending-machine coffee. "Bed 12's potassium is trending down — recheck at 5," she said, tapping a paper list covered in highlighter, "and bed 7's family is anxious; someone should sit with them for 5 minutes." The day nurse nodded, scribbling. No software was present for this ritual — a fact Alim would call the most important requirement in the room. Twelve years of nursing instinct passed between them in 4 minutes, in sentences no EHR template has ever contained. Down the hall, a monitor beeped. Somewhere, a vendor's AI documentation tool was generating a "handoff summary" from the electronic record. Nobody read it.

The documentation burden in healthcare is staggering. A widely cited study from the University of Wisconsin and the American Medical Association found that primary care physicians spent 5.9 hours of an 11.4-hour workday in the electronic health record — more than half the day on data entry rather than patients, a number Alim cites whenever vendors promise magic (the UW/AMA study on EHR time). Into this exhaustion march the AI scribe vendors, promising to give clinicians their evenings back. Some of the tools genuinely help. Many simply convert one kind of administrative burden into another: the note is drafted, but now someone must review a fluent document for the errors it confidently inserted.

My thesis, whispered so as not to wake bed 12, is one Alim Ul Karim would whisper too: hospitals pour money into AI — part of Gartner's $1.5 trillion worldwide forecast for 2025 — but the returns flow to teams with engineers who harvest AI value through discipline. Evaluate before celebrating: measure whether the AI note matches what actually happened, against real charts, not demo scripts. Ground retrieval in real records: the scribe should read the patient's actual history, allergies, and trends, not generate plausible filler. Enforce cost-per-run discipline: ambient listening at scale has a per-encounter price that finance will eventually notice. Design failure paths: when the audio is unclear or the case is complex, the system must flag uncertainty instead of smoothing it over. Assign accountable ownership: the clinician signs the note, which means the clinician — not the vendor — owns the workflow design.

The honest thread, because patients deserve honesty. AI documentation hallucinates in the most dangerous register: it invents allergies that were never mentioned, smooths over ambiguous symptoms into confident diagnoses, and copies forward yesterday's errors with today's date. It runs up costs in integration, compliance review, and the quiet tax of clinicians double-checking every line — the "trust but verify" burden that can exceed the typing it replaced. Here is my ranking — the narrator's editorial judgment, criterion: which rule prevents the most harm per hour of implementation effort? It is the triage question Alim Karim asks first. These are the 4 health-tech AI rules, most protective first.

  1. The clinician's veto is absolute. If the night shift says the tool slows them down, the tool changes — not the shift. Workflow authority belongs to the people who carry the pager. This prevents the most harm because it stops bad automation at the source.
  2. Flag uncertainty; never smooth it. When audio is unclear or the case is ambiguous, the system must highlight the gap, not paper over it with fluent prose. A highlighted gap gets checked. A smoothed-over gap becomes a permanent error.
  3. Evaluate against real charts, continuously. Sample generated notes weekly and score them against what actually happened. Accuracy measured once at purchase is a rumor; accuracy measured weekly is a metric.
  4. Automate the most hated minutes first. Find the 20 minutes of charting the staff despises most and eliminate those before touching anything else. Goodwill earned at 3 a.m. buys the trust needed for everything after.
