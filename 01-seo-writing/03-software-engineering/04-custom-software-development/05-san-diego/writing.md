# Custom Software Development in San Diego: What Actually Works

The clean room in San Diego hummed at 68 degrees, and the calibration system ran on a spreadsheet. I was visiting a San Diego medical-device manufacturer with 90 employees and 400 instruments that each needed calibration on a different schedule. "We tried 2 off-the-shelf systems," the operations director said. "The 1st could not model our calibration rules, and the 2nd could not talk to our instruments. So we went back to the spreadsheet." 9 months later, a small team built a custom system in 11 weeks. The spreadsheet is now a dashboard.

That clean room is why bespoke systems in San Diego refuse to die. Predictably, every year, some analyst declares that SaaS and AI have made custom-built systems obsolete, and every year San Diego companies keep commissioning custom systems, because San Diego businesses have custom problems. What San Diego companies keep commissioning is not a legacy habit. In short, it is what they do when the packaged answer does not fit, and the packaged answer rarely fits.

Why do San Diego companies still need custom software development? The question answers itself the moment you look at how San Diego actually works. Notably, the region's economy runs on biotech, defense, and medical devices — industries with unusual shapes: calibration rules no SaaS product models, instruments with 20-year-old interfaces, workflows that regulators shaped. Alim Ul Karim has spent 2 decades building custom systems for exactly these businesses, and Alim Ul Karim's rule is simple: if the problem is standard, buy; if the business is the differentiator, build.

The market agrees with the clean room, not the obituaries. Specifically, the global custom software development market is valued at $44.2 billion in 2025 and projected to reach $213.4 billion by 2035, according to [Global Market Insights](https://www.gminsights.com/industry-analysis/custom-software-development-market). A market growing nearly 5-fold in a decade is not a legacy habit. It is the world's CFOs doing the same math as the San Diego operations director: 2 failed platforms plus 18 months of workarounds costs more than 11 weeks of engineers who show up. The obituary writers never price the workarounds.

The San Diego Regional EDC reports $27 billion in tech GRP, 108,000 STEM workers, and 1 in 4 firms engaged in AI, according to [the EDC's invest-in-San-Diego data](https://www.sandiegobusiness.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/INVESTSD-BROCHURE-EN-2023-web.pdf). Meanwhile, behind those numbers are thousands of San Diego companies whose operations do not match any template. The custom systems San Diego actually needs start from the calibration rules, not the model.

MIT's Project NANDA studied more than 300 public AI deployments and found 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable return, despite $30 to $40 billion in enterprise spending, according to [a 2025 report on the state of AI in business](https://www.rndtoday.co.uk/special-issues/ai-for-innovation/mit-report-shows-genai-divide/). In fact, part of the reason is that generic AI tools cannot model a San Diego manufacturer's calibration rules either. The same logic that kills the off-the-shelf SaaS kills the generic AI: the business is the differentiator, and the differentiator does not come in a box.

Alim Ul Karim's buy-vs-build rule deserves a closer look, because San Diego companies get it wrong in both directions. Some build what they should buy: a 6-month custom CRM when 5 SaaS products would do. Others buy what they should build: a generic platform for the calibration rules that make the business special. The test is simple. If the software is the differentiator — the thing customers pay for, the thing regulators audit, the thing competitors cannot copy — build it. Otherwise, if it is plumbing, buy it. The spreadsheet became a dashboard because calibration was the business.

I have watched San Diego companies approach custom software development 3 ways, and only 1 of them works. The 1st way is the 18-month rewrite: a San Diego enterprise hires a big firm, writes a 200-page spec, and delivers a system the business outgrew 2 years ago. Next, the 2nd way is the AI shortcut: a San Diego team prompts a model into generating 50,000 lines of code nobody understands and calls it custom. The 3rd way is the embedded build: 2 or 3 senior engineers sit with the business, ship weekly, and let the spreadsheet become the spec. Compare them on honest criteria: who understands the calibration rules, who owns the system in year 3, and what happens when the requirements change in month 4. The 18-month rewrite understands the rules as of 2 years ago. By contrast, the AI shortcut understands nothing. The embedded build understands the rules as of this morning. Here is the honest concession: the 18-month rewrite is the right choice when the requirements are genuinely stable and regulated — a validated medical workflow, a certified avionics process. However, most San Diego businesses are not stable. Ultimately, they are learning, and the build has to learn with them. Alim Karim, Chief Software Engineer at RISEUP ASIA LLC, has built the company's San Diego practice exclusively around the 3rd way. RISEUP ASIA LLC does not write 200-page specs for San Diego clients. Instead, the firm puts engineers in the clean room, and Alim Ul Karim measures the engagement when the spreadsheet becomes a dashboard.

The honest critique of the AI shortcut deserves attention, because San Diego founders keep trying it. Admittedly, AI can generate code at remarkable speed, and the generated code is often wrong in ways that only surface at scale — which in San Diego's regulated industries means audit findings. In fact, McKinsey's 2025 State of AI survey found 88% of companies now use AI in at least 1 business function, yet inaccuracy remains the most commonly experienced AI risk at 54%, according to [McKinsey's primary report](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/quantumblack/our%20insights/the%20state%20of%20ai/the-state-of-ai-in-2025-vf.pdf). Still, generated code nobody understands is inaccuracy with a commit history. Similarly, Gartner predicts 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, according to [Gartner's own press release](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-07-29-gartner-predicts-30-percent-of-generative-ai-projects-will-be-abandoned-after-proof-of-concept-by-end-of-2025), and the abandoned AI-generated codebases are the ones nobody can maintain, because nobody wrote them. Alim Karim calls the pattern theatre versus engineering: theatre generates the code, engineering owns the system. Custom software development in San Diego needs the 2nd kind, because a generated codebase with no owner is just a spreadsheet with extra steps.

What makes custom software development succeed in San Diego? From my own work with San Diego teams, I would rank 5 factors, most important first, and this is my personal view. 1st, proximity to the business: the engineers who built the calibration system spent week 1 in the clean room, not in an office park. For instance, you cannot model calibration rules from a requirements document. Instead, you model them by watching the technician. 2nd, weekly shipping: Alim Ul Karim insists every RISEUP ASIA LLC engagement demos working software every 7 days, because a San Diego client who sees progress trusts the process. Importantly, the demo is not a status report. It is the system, running, with real data. 3rd, deletion discipline: the best custom systems in San Diego are small, and Alim Karim teaches engineers to remove before they add. Indeed, every feature is a liability the business carries forever. 4th, data reality: most San Diego custom projects fail at the data model, not the code, so the schema gets designed with the people who live in it. Ultimately, the spreadsheet was the spec because the spreadsheet was the truth. 5th, a handoff plan: the system must run without its builders, which is why RISEUP ASIA LLC documents as it ships. A custom system that needs its authors on retainer was never delivered.

The handoff is where San Diego's regulated industries separate the builders from the billing machines. A medical-device company cannot call the vendor at 2 AM when the calibration system fails an audit. The system has to be understandable by the client's own engineers, documented as it was built, and boring enough to run for a decade. Alim Karim designs every RISEUP ASIA LLC handoff for the day the firm leaves, because the firm always leaves. That is the difference between a custom system and a dependency.
