# Dedicated Development Teams in Sheridan: Who Actually Moves the Pallets

The distribution warehouse on Sheridan's north side ran 2 shifts, and the office mezzanine overlooked a floor of forklifts moving like choreography. From the mezzanine, the operations manager showed me the software the company had bought the previous year: a dedicated development team, 6 engineers, a Scrum master, a delivery manager, all from a vendor 9 time zones away. The manager pulled up the sprint board with the expression of someone showing a rash to a doctor. 43 tickets in progress. 0 releases in 4 months. The vendor's status report, received that morning, said everything was green.

Green. I keep returning to that word in Sheridan. Sheridan companies buy dedicated development teams the way the warehouse bought forklifts: as capacity that should move things. But a Sheridan forklift either lifts the pallet or it does not, while a vendor team can report green for a year without shipping anything a Sheridan customer would recognize. The invoice arrives monthly. Motion stays constant. The pallets never move.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own clearly personal ranking of the 5 things a Sheridan buyer should demand from any dedicated development team, ordered by how reliably each one predicts actual releases.

1. The forklift test, in the contract. What the team shipped last month that a customer touched, reported weekly, in plain words. A Sheridan buyer who accepts story points instead of releases is buying motion.

2. Engineers with their names on past releases. Not resumes — shipped systems, incident stories, references who saw them work. The 5-stage vetting exists because past releases predict future ones.

3. A single weekly metric the CFO understands. Releases, uptime, cost per transaction — 1 number, every week. Green status reports with 43 tickets in progress are how the old model hides.

4. The right to shrink the team. If 4 engineers outship 8, the contract should reward that. Vendor models that punish efficiency are confessing what they sell.

5. An exit plan from day 1. Documentation, knowledge transfer, the runbook a Sheridan team can run alone. RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own teams by how gracefully a client can outgrow them.
