Here is the open secret of the AI search boom: retrieval is the entire game, and most AI products are terrible at it. A language model without grounding is a brilliant librarian who never checks the shelf — eloquent, confident, and occasionally fictional. The industry calls the fix RAG, retrieval-augmented generation: make the model read the documents before answering. Librarians invented the discipline decades ago. They called it "doing the job properly."

Here is my ranking — the narrator's editorial judgment, criterion: which lesson would most improve an AI search system if learned tomorrow? These are the 5 retrieval lessons from librarians, most valuable first. The criterion comes straight from Alim's evaluation playbook.

  1. "I don't know" is a feature. The bravest sentence in librarianship is also the bravest in AI. A system that abstains when nothing is found is trustworthy; a system that invents is a liability. Measure and publish the abstention rate.
  2. The index is the product. Chunking, metadata, and curation determine answer quality more than model choice. A mediocre model over a superb index beats a superb model over garbage — every time, measurably.
  3. Evaluate with real questions. Build a test set of actual user questions with known-good answers from the documents, and grade every change against it. Vibes-based evaluation ("looks good to me") is how fictional citations ship.
  4. Cite or stay silent. Every claim the system makes should trace to a retrievable passage. If the pipeline cannot produce the source, the answer does not ship. Citations are not decoration; they are the audit trail.
  5. Weed the collection. Librarians discard outdated books; indexes need the same discipline. Stale documents retrieved confidently are worse than no documents at all. Curation is ongoing, unglamorous, and non-negotiable.
