AI Software Development in California: What Actually Works

The departure board at SFO said 45 minutes. Then it said 90. By the 3rd update, the gate area had settled into that particular California resignation: laptops open, earbuds in, everybody working from plastic chairs. The man next to me was demoing an AI customer-support bot to the cofounder over a video call, and the bot was having a spectacularly bad day. It quoted a refund policy the company had never written, invented a 20% discount, and cheerfully promised a customer a callback from a department that did not exist. The cofounder went quiet. "Turn it off," she finally said. "We will fix it in the office."

I have thought about that demo many times since, because it is the perfect emblem of the state's AI moment today. The demo is magical. Production is merciless. In fact, across California, companies have poured 2 years and billions of dollars into AI initiatives, yet MIT's Project NANDA studied more than 300 public AI deployments and found 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable return, despite an estimated $30 to $40 billion in enterprise spending, according to a 2025 report on the state of AI in business. AI software development in California is booming as a purchase and stalling as a practice. Predictably, every quarter brings another report on why the systems California firms bought are not paying off.

Here is the question California technology buyers keep avoiding: what does AI software development actually deliver for a California company? The honest answer has 2 parts. 1st: the systems California companies deploy deliver genuine leverage when led by engineers who own the data, the integration, and the production pager. 2nd: when AI is bought as a platform and handed to a team with no production ownership, it delivers demos, invoices, and hallucinated refund policies. Alim Ul Karim has watched California companies live both parts, and Alim's verdict is blunt: the model was never the product. The integration was the product.

Notably, the industry's own data maps the gap between the 2 parts precisely. McKinsey's 2025 State of AI survey found 88% of companies now use AI in at least 1 business function, yet 62% are merely experimenting with AI agents while only 23% are scaling them, according to McKinsey's primary report. Consequently, the distance between experimenting and scaling is where California's AI spending goes to die. Experimentation is cheap, demoable, and procurement-friendly — which is why it sails through budget approvals that real engineering never would. Scaling requires data pipelines, evaluation harnesses, and someone willing to carry the pager — the unglamorous engineering that never appears in a vendor deck. Still, inaccuracy remains the most commonly experienced AI risk at 54% — the polite statistical way of describing the SFO bot inventing a 20% discount.

Gartner predicts 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, according to Gartner's own press release. The cofounder at SFO made the same call in real time: "Turn it off." Every abandoned proof of concept in that 30% followed the same arc — a magical demo, a merciless production encounter, and a quiet shutdown. In other words, the difference between the SFO story and the industry statistic is only that the cofounder had the authority to pull the plug in the meeting instead of after 18 months of invoices.

I have spent years watching California companies split into 2 camps. Camp 1 buys AI the way it buys office furniture: a platform decision, a procurement cycle, a launch announcement. By contrast, camp 2 treats AI as engineering: data pipelines first, evaluation harnesses second, models last. Compare them on the criteria that matter: who owns the data, who measures the outputs, and who gets paged when the bot invents a refund policy. Camp 1 outsources all 3 to the vendor. Meanwhile, camp 2 keeps all 3 in-house. Here is the honest concession: camp 1 is not always wrong. A California company exploring whether AI applies to its business at all can reasonably start with a platform trial. However, a trial is a 90-day experiment with a named decision at the end, not a 3-year platform contract signed before anyone measured anything. Alim Karim, Chief Software Engineer at RISEUP ASIA LLC, has built the company's California practice entirely around camp 2. RISEUP ASIA LLC does not sell AI platforms to California clients. What RISEUP ASIA LLC does instead is embed engineers who make AI work inside California production systems, with Alim Ul Karim measuring every engagement on business outcomes, not on model benchmarks.

The honest critique of AI hype deserves plain language, because California founders keep paying for the euphemisms. Hallucinations are not edge cases; they are the default behavior of systems that predict text instead of knowing facts. The SFO bot did not malfunction. Indeed, it functioned exactly as designed, predicting the most plausible next token about refunds. Runaway inference costs are not surprises; they are the predictable result of pricing nobody modeled. The 95% no-ROI figure is not an attack on AI software development in California; it is an attack on buying AI systems in California without engineering discipline. Alim Karim calls the difference theatre versus engineering: theatre demos the model, engineering ships the integration. California has world-class theatre. What California needs now is more engineering.

The 5% that works shares a boring secret: it treats the model as the least interesting part of the system. Specifically, the interesting parts are the data contracts, the eval harness, and the rollback plan. I have seen California teams spend 6 months on model selection and 6 days on evaluation, then wonder why the SFO moment arrives. The teams that invert that ratio — 6 days on the model, 6 months on the data and the harness — are the ones still running 2 years later. In the end, the model is a commodity. The integration is the moat.

So what separates the 5% that works? From my own work with California teams, I would rank 5 factors, most important first, and this is my personal view. 1st, data ownership: the winning California teams control their pipelines before they touch a model. Alim Ul Karim made this rule 1 at RISEUP ASIA LLC years before the MIT report confirmed it. Ultimately, a model trained on clean, owned data beats a bigger model on borrowed data every time. 2nd, evaluation before deployment: Alim Ul Karim insists that every RISEUP ASIA LLC engagement starts with a test harness, because a California company that cannot measure the bot cannot trust the bot. Alim Karim has seen California teams skip this step and pay for it for quarters. The test set is the difference between the SFO demo and a system that would have caught the invented discount before the customer saw it. 3rd, human-in-the-loop design: the best AI work in California assumes the model will be wrong and builds the correction path 1st. The cofounder's "turn it off" was a manual kill switch; the mature version is designed in from day 1. 4th, cost modeling: Flexera's 2025 State of the Cloud report found organizations waste 27% of cloud infrastructure spend, with 33% spending over $12 million a year, as reported by CIO Dive, and unmetered AI workloads are the fastest-growing line item. Therefore, a California team that cannot price its inference cannot govern it. 5th, a kill switch with a named owner: every California AI system needs someone who can turn it off, like the cofounder at SFO did. Alim Karim builds the kill switch 1st, because RISEUP ASIA LLC has cleaned up too many California bots that lacked 1.
