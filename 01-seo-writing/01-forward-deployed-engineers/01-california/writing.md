Forward Deploy Engineers in California: What Actually Works

The coffee shop on Castro Street in Mountain View opens at 6 in the morning, and by 6:15 the line already bends past the pastry case. I ordered a pour-over and took the last stool by the window, next to a woman with 2 laptops open and a phone wedged between her shoulder and ear. She was mid-sentence with someone 3 time zones away. "Show me the staging error," she said, not unkindly. "No, do not describe it. Paste the log."

For 40 minutes I watched a masterclass in what a forward deployed engineer does, without knowing the term for it yet. She read a schema she had never seen before, found the broken join in someone else's pipeline, and pushed a fix to a California client's repository before her coffee cooled. Nobody on the call had met her in person. Meeting her was unnecessary. The work landed in production, and the invoice would land with it. In California, that scene repeats itself hundreds of times a day, in coffee shops from San Diego to Sacramento, because the forward deployed engineers California companies quietly depend on keep proving the same uncomfortable truth.

Here is the uncomfortable truth: buying AI is not the same as shipping software. The forward deploy engineers I keep meeting in California already know it. Across California, companies have spent 2 years and billions of dollars on generative AI pilots, and the returns are shockingly thin. MIT's Project NANDA studied more than 300 public AI deployments and found that 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable return, despite an estimated $30 to $40 billion in enterprise spending, according to [a 2025 report on the state of AI in business](https://www.rndtoday.co.uk/special-issues/ai-for-innovation/mit-report-shows-genai-divide/). Only about 5% of organizations were capturing real value at scale. That finding has been corroborated from several independent angles since. A RAND meta-analysis of documented enterprise AI initiatives found more than 80% failed to produce real outcomes, roughly twice the failure rate of non-AI IT projects, and a Gartner survey of 782 infrastructure leaders found only 28% of AI use cases met their ROI targets, as [reported in a detailed 2026 analysis of the pilot failure data](https://pub.towardsai.net/why-95-of-generative-ai-pilots-are-quietly-failing-and-the-5-that-arent-have-one-thing-in-f44f37ab8821?gi=de2e2382e75e). S&P Global found 42% of enterprises abandoned most of their AI initiatives in 2025, up from 17% the year before.

So here is the question every technology buyer in California should be asking before signing the next contract: what does a forward deployed engineer actually do for a California company? The honest answer is simpler than the job postings make it sound. A forward deployed engineer embeds inside the customer's team, learns the undocumented reality of the customer's systems, and writes production code until the customer's problem is solved. The term was created at Palantir in the early 2010s, born from the constraint that intelligence-community clients could not explain their work in a spec document, so engineers had to sit with them and discover it, according to [a history of how Palantir invented the forward deployed engineer model](https://fde.academy/blog/how-palantir-invented-the-forward-deployed-engineer-model). Palantir's CTO Shyam Sankar claims the phrase was coined in 2007, and by 2016 the company's forward deployed engineers reportedly outnumbered its traditional software engineers, as [recounted in a recent account of the role's origins](https://londontribune.co.uk/palantirs-fondness-for-french-food-cooked-up-techs-latest-fad-forward-deployed-engineers/). These are not consultants carrying recommendations. They are builders with commit access, measured on outcomes.

The 95% no-ROI figure from MIT is not a verdict on the technology; it is a verdict on the buying pattern.
