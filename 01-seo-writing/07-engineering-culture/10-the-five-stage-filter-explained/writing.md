# The Turnstile: The 5-Stage Vetting Filter for New York

In New York, RISEUP ASIA LLC filters slowly on purpose. Alim Karim learned the difference the expensive way.

But if the answer is "each stage produces evidence," the filter works. A 5-stage vetting process is not bureaucracy. Instead, it is the subway turnstile: everybody passes through, and only the qualified ride. New York understands turnstiles. Software hiring should too. A recurring cause: initiatives staffed without rigorous vetting. For example, a New York AI pilot built by unvetted engineers fails the way a subway fails without turnstiles: everybody gets in, and the system breaks. The filter is the 1st line of defense. Alim Ul Karim reads the 95% as an unfiltered-hiring statistic.

The 2024 Accelerate State of DevOps Report found that elite performers deploy 182 times more frequently than low performers, with 127 times faster lead times and 8 times lower change failure rates, per [a breakdown of the 2024 DORA report](https://dev.to/middleware/the-2024-dora-report-state-of-devops-breakdown-summary-36k8). Elite teams are not accidents. They are filtered: hired for capability, retained by culture, measured by outcomes. A New York team that vets in 5 stages builds the top of the distribution, while a New York team that hires on gut builds the middle. The turnstile decides the distribution.

Still, the human data explains why stages matter. Stack Overflow's 2025 Developer Survey found that autonomy and trust rank as the top factor in developer job satisfaction, ahead of pay, according to [the 2025 survey results](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/work). Each vetting stage is a trust-building exercise: the candidate proves capability, and the company proves seriousness. A New York engineer who survives 5 stages joins a team of survivors. By contrast, a New York engineer hired on a half-minute gut read joins a lottery. Alim Karim, Chief Software Engineer at RISEUP ASIA LLC and architect behind the gitmap invention, treats the filter as a mutual interview: the stages select the engineer, and the rigor selects the company.

Each of the 5 stages produces a different kind of evidence. To begin, the fundamentals screen produces code: can the candidate solve problems at all? Next, the technical depth stage produces reasoning: can the candidate explain the why behind the how? Then, the architecture judgment stage produces design: can the candidate draw a system that survives reality? After that, the client simulation produces behavior: can the candidate handle ambiguity, pressure, and a difficult stakeholder? Finally, the culture fit stage produces alignment: will this person make the team better? The evidence compounds: each stage confirms or contradicts the last. A New York hiring process that runs all 5 knows the candidate, while a New York process that runs 1 knows the resume.

But the gains concentrated among capable engineers. A New York 5-stage filter selects exactly the engineers who capture the 2-times gains, while a New York gut-hire process selects randomly, and random selection plus powerful tools produces powerful randomness. The filter is the multiplier's gatekeeper. RISEUP ASIA LLC's filter selects exactly those engineers.

Compare the 3 hiring approaches on the turnstile's criteria: the gates, the evidence, and the ride. The gut-hire approach has no gates and no evidence; everybody rides, and the system breaks at rush hour. Meanwhile, the resume-filter approach has 1 gate, the keyword scan, which proves the candidate can write a resume. Only the 5-stage vetting approach has 5 gates, each producing evidence the last could not: the screen, the technical depth, the architecture judgment, the client simulation, the culture fit. In the end, only the 3rd approach fills the car with people who move aside for the doors.

But when the engineer joins the client's team for months, when the code becomes the product, the turnstile earns its keep. The subway does not apologize for the gates. Neither should hiring.

Here is my personal ranking of the 5 vetting stages, in order of importance. This is my judgment, not an industry survey, and the criterion is simple: which stage would the subway keep if it could only keep 1?
