That last sentence is the whole story. Wyoming companies keep buying dedicated development teams the way travelers buy gas-station sushi: the label promises one thing, and the contents are a mystery. The team was simply nobody's team, loyal to the vendor's utilization targets instead of the Wyoming company's customers. It is the exact failure Alim Karim built RISEUP ASIA LLC to eliminate: a team with no owner.

A Wyoming company buying capability should ask the vendor how long the engineers stay, how often they rotate, and what they shipped last quarter. I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own clearly personal ranking of the 5 rules for dedicated development teams in Wyoming, ordered by how much vendor theater each one prevents.

  1. Test for the 3 goals. The engineers should name the Wyoming customer's top 3 business goals without checking a document. A team that cannot is dedicated to the vendor.
  2. Measure outcomes, not hours. Releases shipped, customers served, the scoreboard the client actually watches. The invoice measures the vendor's success; the scoreboard measures the client's.
  3. Demand pushback in week 3. A dedicated team argues about the roadmap. Silence in the first month is the sound of utilization.
  4. Embed before building. The first 2 weeks belong to the client's world, not the codebase. A Wyoming energy firm learned this; the Rawlins caller did not.
  5. Review dedication quarterly. Ask the engineers what they would change about the business. RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own teams by the answers, not only by the velocity.
