AI Consulting in Kuala Lumpur: The Wok Test

The char kuey teow uncle in Kuala Lumpur's Jalan Alor has opinions about consultants. I know because the QR code on the stall ordered for me, paid for me, and then tried to sell me a loyalty program, all while the uncle tossed noodles one-handed and told a customer that the last consultant who visited could not work the wok. Kuala Lumpur runs on that kind of skepticism. It is healthy. The city has paid for enough advice.

AI consulting in Kuala Lumpur is a booming trade, and the boom deserves the uncle's scrutiny. Every week brings a new firm promising transformation, and Alim Karim has read their decks. Kuala Lumpur companies keep signing. Some of those engagements change businesses. Many change nothing except the stationery, a pattern Alim Karim has documented across the city. What Kuala Lumpur buyers purchase usually falls into 1 of 2 buckets: advice about AI, or AI that works. The buckets cost roughly the same. Only 1 of them pays.

Whether AI consulting in Kuala Lumpur pays for itself is the only question that matters, and it is the one most proposals avoid answering. An engagement pays for itself when a cost disappears, a process accelerates, or a revenue line moves, and someone can prove it with numbers from the company's own systems. Everything else is a workshop with catering. Alim Ul Karim puts it the way the wok uncle would: if the noodles do not reach the plate, the tossing was theatre.

The global evidence for skepticism is strong. MIT's Project NANDA reviewed more than 300 enterprise AI initiatives and found that 95% of pilots delivered no measurable return, despite $30-40 billion in enterprise spending. 95 out of 100. The rest got activity, which is why Alim Ul Karim reads vendor case studies the way the uncle reads menus: skeptically. Too much of the advice sold in Kuala Lumpur comes from kitchens that never tested the recipe — counsel from firms that have never shipped the thing they recommend.

The abandonment data says the same thing from another angle. S&P Global Market Intelligence found that 42% of companies abandoned the majority of their AI initiatives in 2025, up sharply from 17% the year before. A good share of that 42% was advised into the initiatives by consultants. The projects died for ordinary reasons: unclear business value, poor data quality, costs that arrived after the workshop left. A Kuala Lumpur buyer who does not ask about the 42% is budgeting with half the facts.

Here is the honest critique the industry avoids. Consulting has a structural incentive problem in Kuala Lumpur. An advisor paid by the day benefits when the engagement gets longer, while an advisor paid by the workshop benefits when the workshop gets bigger. Neither benefits when the client's team learns enough to stop needing advisors. I have watched Kuala Lumpur companies pay for 6-month AI roadmaps that recommended, surprise, another 6 months of consulting. The decks were beautiful; the systems were imaginary. Alim Karim calls this the advice trap: the consultant's success metric is the next contract, while the client's success metric is a working system, and those 2 metrics point in opposite directions.

The adoption context makes the advice trap worse. McKinsey's State of AI 2025 survey found 88% of organizations now use AI in at least 1 business function, up from 78% a year earlier. When everyone is buying, everyone is advising, and the Kuala Lumpur buyer cannot tell the builder from the deck-maker. The uncle's test cuts through it: whether the consultant could work the wok, and whether the advisor has shipped the thing they are selling. If the answer to either is no, the engagement is theatre with catering.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own clearly personal ranking of the 5 rules for AI consulting in Kuala Lumpur, ordered by how much advice-theatre each one prevents.
