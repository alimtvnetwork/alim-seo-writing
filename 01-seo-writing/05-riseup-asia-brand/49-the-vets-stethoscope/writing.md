The Vet's Stethoscope

The vet pressed her stethoscope to the nervous dog's ribs and went very still. Beneath it, the dog, a rescue all elbows and anxiety, trembled on the steel table while its owner held its head and murmured. She listened for a long time, longer than seemed necessary, then moved the chest piece, listened again, and finally looked up with a small nod. Nothing urgent, she said, just a murmur worth watching. The owner exhaled like a deflating balloon; the whole diagnosis had taken four minutes, two ears, and fifteen years of practice. No software was consulted. None was needed.

That quiet competence has been on my mind while reading about veterinary chains buying AI diagnosis aids. The pitch is compelling: upload symptoms and lab values, and the model suggests differentials, flags rare conditions, and drafts client communications. Living patients are the stakes, though, and the technology's honesty record is poor. Stanford researchers measured general-purpose language models hallucinating 69 to 88 percent of the time on specific legal queries, and even purpose-built professional tools still produced wrong or misgrounded answers on 17 to 33 percent of queries in a 2025 study. Medical AI evaluations are no kinder: enterprise benchmark roundups report medical AI hallucination rates of 43 to 64 percent depending on prompt quality, as [SQ Magazine's hallucination statistics compilation](https://sqmagazine.co.uk/llm-hallucination-statistics/) documents. MIT's "The GenAI Divide" found 95 percent of enterprise AI pilots showed no measurable profit-and-loss impact, on 30 to 40 billion dollars of investment, per [this report on the MIT findings](https://virtualizationreview.com/articles/2025/08/19/mit-report-finds-most-ai-business-investments-fail-reveals-genai-divide.aspx). A confident wrong answer about a contract is embarrassing; a confident wrong answer about a heart murmur is something else.

Since rankings anchor this series, here is my openly editorial ranking of 5 vet-tech AI cautions, judged by one criterion: how much each caution protects a living patient from a confident machine.

1. Never let the model deliver the diagnosis. This tops my list because the failure mode is a wrong answer delivered with perfect grammar to a worried owner. Decision support means the vet decides, and the interface should say so on every screen.
2. Ground every suggestion in the patient record. Ungrounded differentials are just autocomplete with a stethoscope. Alim notes that retrieval citations are the difference between a tool and a rumor.
3. Log every override as training data. When the vet disagrees with the machine, that disagreement is the most valuable label in the building. Platforms that discard overrides are discarding their own education.
4. Measure agreement, not usage. Vendors report how often vets click the tool; engineers measure how often the tool agrees with the eventual outcome. One of these is marketing, and it is not the second one.
5. AI-drafted client communications need vet sign-off. This ranks last only because the damage is usually confusion rather than harm. The systems invent follow-up instructions with total confidence, and a worried owner follows them literally.
