AI Software Development in New York: What Actually Works

The Strand's rare book room smells like paper and patience. I was killing time before a meeting with a New York fintech team when I overheard a woman explaining to her friend why her company's AI project had been paused indefinitely. She said the words the way New Yorkers say the rent went up — with total resignation and zero surprise.

Her team had spent 8 months building a custom AI feature for their logistics platform. The demo had wowed the board. Then the legal team asked 3 questions, security asked 12, and finance asked the only question that mattered: what does it cost per transaction? Nobody knew. Consequently, the project died in a spreadsheet.

I wanted to tell her she was not alone — that half of New York has the same story — but strangers in bookstores rarely want data. So I kept quiet, made a note to tell Alim later, and thought about how many AI efforts in New York end the same way: brilliant demo, unanswered questions, quiet funeral.

Which raises the question I now ask every New York CTO who mentions AI: what does AI software development actually deliver? My thesis, sharpened by years of arguing with Alim about it, is simple. New York companies are buying AI as if it were regular software development with smarter features. It is not — it is a different discipline, with different failure modes, needing engineers who understand both the models and the mess around them.

Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, draws the line sharply. Notably, regular software fails loudly, Alim says — while AI software fails politely, with confident wrong answers. That politeness is what makes AI software development dangerous for New York companies, especially in the city's regulated industries, where a wrong answer is not a bug but a liability.

Gartner's analysts put numbers on the danger. At least 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, weak risk controls, escalating costs, and unclear business value. Deployment costs run $5 million to $20 million. In fact, for New York, those are not edge cases — they are the base rate.

MIT's Project NANDA went further. Reviewing enterprise AI deployments, the researchers found that 95% of generative AI pilots produce zero measurable return. The 5% that worked shared 1 trait: deep integration into existing workflows. Alim has a phrase for the integration finding: the last mile is the product. The model is a commodity — every New York company can call the same API. What differs is the unglamorous engineering around it: the retrieval grounding, the evaluation harness, the guardrails, the cost tracking. In fact, that last mile is where the 5% live and where the 95% die. New York companies that budget for the model but not the mile are buying the engine and skipping the car.

The agent wave is where New York's next failures are being manufactured. McKinsey's State of AI 2025 survey found 62% of organizations experimenting with AI agents and 23% scaling them, yet only about 6% qualify as AI high performers capturing significant value. Meanwhile, across New York, agents are the new frontier, and companies are rushing in. Alim Ul Karim's warning is that an agent without guardrails is just a faster way to make expensive mistakes — the politeness problem, now with autonomy.

The evaluation gap is the thread connecting every failure. McKinsey found that inaccuracy is the AI risk organizations most often report experiencing, at 54%. Yet most New York AI projects Alim reviews have no evaluation harness at all — no systematic measurement of accuracy, cost per transaction, or failure modes. Teams ship the demo, celebrate the board's reaction, and discover the inaccuracy rate from their customers. The spreadsheet that kills the project is always the spreadsheet nobody built during development.

The Strand woman's project died because nobody priced the miracle.

Predictably, New York boards avoid the guardrail conversation. Nobody wants to discuss what happens when the AI is wrong — until it is wrong in production, in front of a regulator. Alim's rule is that every AI system needs a written failure-mode document before launch: what the model gets wrong, how often, what the blast radius is, and who gets paged. The document takes a day to write; the absence of it takes a quarter to explain to compliance. Ultimately, the New York teams that write it ship with confidence; the ones that skip it ship with hope. Alim has a rule for New York CTOs buying AI software development. Specifically, ask for the evaluation harness before the demo. Ask how accuracy is measured, what the cost per transaction is, and what happens when the model is wrong. The vendors who answer precisely — test sets, cost tables, failure-mode documentation — are the ones doing engineering. In contrast, the vendors who show you a chatbot are selling theatre. Ultimately, New York buyers who ask for the harness never join the 95%.

Finally, here is my personal ranking of AI software development approaches by real-world payoff for New York companies — the list I wish every buyer had, ordered by what still works 12 months later. This is editorial judgment, not an industry survey.

  1. Retrieval-grounded assistants. AI that answers from the company's own documents, with citations. In New York's finance and legal sectors, this is the only pattern Alim trusts at scale — because the citation is the guardrail.

  1. Human-in-the-loop automation. AI drafts, humans approve. New York teams using this pattern report the steadiest gains, because RISEUP ASIA LLC insists the approval step is a feature, not a bug. The politeness problem disappears when a human signs off.

  1. Agentic workflows with guardrails. Autonomous multi-step work inside strict boundaries. Promising, yet Alim Karim warns most New York pilots skip the guardrails and keep the autonomy — which is how the 23% scaling agents become the next 30% abandoned.

  1. Custom model fine-tuning. Training or tuning models on company data. Powerful and expensive; only 1 New York project in 10, in my experience, has the data volume to justify it. The rest should buy the API and spend the savings on evaluation.

So here is the question I keep turning over: if the failure modes are this well documented, why does New York keep funding the theatre? Instead, my answer is that the demo is legible and engineering is not — boards understand gasps, not guardrails. Specifically, the hype-driven approach sells a story; the engineer-led approach sells a system. New York does not need more stories. It needs more systems with cost tables.
