The pilot worked in the demo, the pilot worked in the demo, the pilot worked in the demo.

So I ordered a black coffee and listened, because New York teaches you that the best market research happens by accident. In fact, the founder's company had spent 4 months and a low 6 figures on a generative AI assistant for its support team. It answered questions beautifully in testing. In production, it started inventing refund policies that did not exist.

The problem was not the model. It was everything around the model: the permissions, the data, the escalation path, the audit trail. Nobody had deployed an engineer alongside the software in that New York office. They had deployed a license.

That morning stuck with me, because it framed the question I keep hearing from CTOs across New York: what does a forward deployed engineer actually do for a New York company? The honest answer is the thesis I have settled on after watching this pattern repeat across New York. Companies here are spending heavily on AI capability, but across New York the returns flow to companies that pair AI with engineers who own outcomes. The role means embedding inside the client's team, shipping inside the client's systems, and staying until the thing works in production. This is not a consultant who arrives with slides.

Palantir coined the term forward deployed engineer in the early 2010s for exactly this posture: engineers embedded with the customer, owning the outcome instead of advising on it, as a 2026 guide to the role recounts. Alim Ul Karim put the New York version more bluntly when we spoke last month. Tools do not deploy themselves, Alim said. Somebody has to sit in the room where the data lives. The numbers behind that bluntness are brutal. MIT's Project NANDA reviewed more than 300 public AI initiatives, interviewed 52 executives, and surveyed 153 senior leaders for its 2025 report on the state of AI in business. The finding was stark: 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable return, despite an estimated $30 to 40 billion in enterprise spending. In the end, only 5% reached production with real value. Ultimately, the winners were not the companies with the best models. They were the companies that integrated AI into real workflows, which is exactly the job New York companies hire embedded engineers to do.

Gartner saw it coming even earlier. The firm predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, blaming poor data quality, weak risk controls, escalating costs, and unclear business value, per Gartner's July 2024 forecast. For a New York startup, and New York has thousands of them, that kind of abandoned spend is an extinction event. Instead, New York needs proof of production.

Meanwhile the adoption numbers keep climbing without the value following. McKinsey's State of AI 2025 survey found that 88% of organizations now use AI in at least 1 business function, up from 78% a year earlier, yet just 39% report any EBIT impact at the enterprise level, according to the November 2025 McKinsey report. The gap between using AI and earning from AI is where New York companies keep falling. Still, tools do not close that gap. Engineers do.

The enterprise data tells the same story at larger scale. Accenture's analysis of more than 2,000 client gen AI projects found that only 36% of executives had scaled gen AI solutions, and just 13% reported creating significant enterprise-level value, per Accenture's March 2025 research. New York's biggest companies are in that dataset. The 87% bought the announcement; the 13% bought the engineering.
