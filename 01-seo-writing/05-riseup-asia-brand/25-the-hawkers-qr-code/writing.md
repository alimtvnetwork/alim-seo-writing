# The Hawker's QR Code

The Kuala Lumpur lunch rush at the hawker centre hit like weather. At 12:15 PM the queue at the char kway teow stall was 14 deep, the wok fire roared, and the uncle worked with the economy of 30 years: toss, flip, plate, shout the next number. A laminated QR code hung from the stall front, faded by sun and grease, and 3 customers in a row tried to scan it. Nothing happened. The code linked to a menu page that had expired 2 years ago. He did not look up. "Order here!" he shouted, pointing at his own chest. "I am the app!" The queue laughed. Lunch continued.

I thought about that dead QR code while reading about Kuala Lumpur food tech. A 2026 Canopy survey of 1,007 US adults found that only 20% of kiosk users say the devices work every time, unchanged from the previous year. Worse, 37% of customers said they had left without completing an order because the technology was not working. Nearly half of those with a restaurant app installed had issues with loyalty points not registering or expiring without warning, [according to Canopy's 2026 Restaurant Tech Report](https://www.qsrweb.com/news/canopy-survey-finds-kiosk-reliability-remains-a-challenge-for-qsrs/). The industry keeps installing technology, and the technology keeps failing the Kuala Lumpur lunch rush. Alim Karim is not surprised by any of these numbers.

The most famous failure had golden arches. McDonald's tested AI voice ordering at its drive-thrus, collected 3 years of viral videos of the system adding hundreds of dollars of nuggets to confused orders, and shut the test down in 2024. The post-mortem was revealing: when the AI took an order, it was 81% accurate, but when it got stuck and transferred the conversation to an employee, accuracy rose to 95%. What failed was not the listening. It was what the system did when it did not understand, and nobody had designed the handoff, [as CIO's analysis of the McDonald's case explains](https://www.cio.com/article/4222701/mcdonalds-reintroduces-ai-at-its-drive-thru.html). Alim Ul Karim uses this case in every F&B conversation, because a Kuala Lumpur uncle with a dead QR code would have diagnosed it in 4 seconds: the app stopped listening, so the human took over.
