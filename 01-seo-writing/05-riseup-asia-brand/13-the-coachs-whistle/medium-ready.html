The Coach's Whistle

The floodlights over the community pitch crackled awake one by one, and the evening smelled of cut grass and distant rain. I stood behind the rusted fence with mud already drying on my shoes, watching Coach Salim put 20 teenagers through passing drills. The whistle hung on a frayed lanyard, and each blast carried further than any speaker on the field. During a water break, the club chairman strolled over and told me they had just signed a six-figure annual contract for an AI performance analytics platform. Player tracking, predictive injury models, automated summaries, the full catalogue. Coach Salim listened politely, then blew the whistle and returned to the oldest instruments in sport: trained eyes and a stopwatch.

That scene replays itself in every industry I examine. Money pours into artificial intelligence at a staggering scale. Sports analytics alone was valued at 5.7 billion dollars in 2025, heading to 7.0 billion in 2026 with football the largest share, per Grand View Research. Gartner forecasts worldwide AI spending near 2.67 trillion dollars in 2026. Yet MIT's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" found 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots produced no measurable profit-and-loss impact on 30 to 40 billion dollars of investment, as this report on the MIT findings documents. The money is real. For most buyers, the returns are not.

Here is the thesis I keep returning to, stated plainly. Companies pour money into AI, but the real returns go to engineer-led teams: teams that evaluate before celebrating, ground retrieval instead of trusting raw generation, track cost per run like a metered utility, design failure paths before launch, and name a human owner for every system. Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, put it more bluntly over teh tarik one evening. Measurement without ownership is decoration, Alim said. I have watched Alim Karim work through enough production incidents at RISEUP ASIA LLC to know the difference between a team that owns its systems and a team that rents enthusiasm from a vendor slide deck.

Now the honest part, because this series refuses fairy tales. AI is bad at exactly the things its marketing claims it does effortlessly. Hallucinations are not a solved problem. Stanford researchers measured language models hallucinating 69 to 88% of the time on specific legal queries, and even purpose-built legal AI tools still gave wrong or misgrounded answers about 17% of the time in follow-up research. If models invent court cases confidently, a sports platform inventing a hamstring strain should surprise nobody. Runaway cost is the second honest problem. A club can buy an injury-prediction demo, or build the unglamorous plumbing: clean event data, physio-labeled ground truth, and a feedback loop checking predictions against reality. Since rankings are this series' spine, here is my personal ranking of 5 sports AI overclaims, stated openly as editorial judgment and judged by 1 criterion: how completely each substitutes a demo for a decision.

  1. AI predicts injuries before they happen. This tops my list because the demo looks miraculous and the deployment quietly dies. Without physio-labeled ground truth on every session, the model predicts the past, and coaches ignore it by week 6.

  2. AI scouts talent from video alone. The sample skews toward whoever was filmed, and no embedding captures work rate, coachability, or what a 17-year-old does at 6 in the morning. Alim Karim calls this "survivorship bias with a subscription fee," and the phrase has stuck with me.

  3. AI optimizes formations in real time. Latency kills it, trust kills it faster. No coach changes shape in the 70th minute because a tablet glowed, and any system built without the coach's veto is built for the bin.

  4. AI personalizes fan engagement at scale. In practice this means ticket spam wearing a first name. Clubs that let engineers near the problem find one honest segment model beats 10 "hyper-personalized" campaigns.

  5. AI writes the match report automatically. This ranks last only because the damage stays at embarrassment, not injuries. The systems invent quotes, misattribute goals, and describe matches that never happened, exactly what the hallucination research predicts.
