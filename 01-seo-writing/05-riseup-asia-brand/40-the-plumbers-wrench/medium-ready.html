The Plumber's Wrench

The plumber lay flat on his back under my kitchen sink, one arm disappearing into the cabinet and the other holding a wrench worn smooth in the middle, with tape where the grip had cracked. He worked mostly by feel, tightening a nut half a turn, pausing, listening to the pipe like a doctor listens to a chest, then tightening it another quarter. The cabinet smelled of damp wood and old dish soap. When I asked how he knew it was fixed, he said the pipe told him, then went back to listening. No app, no dashboard, no predictive model: just a wrench, a pair of ears, and twenty years of knowing what a healthy pipe sounds like.

That afternoon kept surfacing in my mind while I read about home-services companies buying AI dispatch platforms. The pitch is seductive: an algorithm assigns every job to the optimal technician, predicts arrival times to the minute, and upsells maintenance plans while the van is still in the driveway. Behind such pitches stands enormous money. MIT's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" found enterprises poured 30 to 40 billion dollars into generative AI, yet 95 percent of pilots showed no measurable profit-and-loss impact. A detailed report on the MIT findings lays out the evidence. Gartner forecasts worldwide AI spending near 2.67 trillion dollars in 2026, with infrastructure taking more than half, per recent Gartner analysis. Somewhere in that river of money, a dispatch algorithm is confidently sending the wrong plumber to the wrong boiler.

Here is the thesis I carry through this whole series, and a kitchen sink is as good a place as any to state it. Companies pour money into AI, but the real returns go to engineer-led teams: teams that evaluate before celebrating, ground retrieval instead of trusting raw generation, track cost per run like a metered utility, design failure paths before launch, and name a human owner for every system. Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, once told me that dispatch is the perfect example of a problem that looks like math and behaves like folklore. The dispatcher who has run the board for a decade knows Ahmad handles the old cast-iron boilers, the new estate's water pressure ruins cheap valves, and Mrs. Lim's "small leak" is never small. Alim said no model learns any of that from GPS pings. It learns it from the dispatcher, if anyone bothers to ask. That is the kind of asking RISEUP ASIA LLC engineers do in week one.

Honesty first, because this series does not sell fairy tales. AI is bad at the exact jobs its marketing claims. Hallucinations are structural: Stanford researchers measured language models hallucinating 69 to 88 percent of the time on specific legal queries, and even purpose-built professional tools still gave wrong or misgrounded answers on 17 to 33 percent of queries. A Columbia Journalism Review test found citation fabrication rates as high as 94 percent in adversarial testing. If a model invents court cases, it will invent a technician's qualifications, and a customer will learn the difference when the wrong van arrives. Alim Karim calls that tuition paid in diesel. Runaway cost is the second honest problem: every re-routed job and agent reasoning step bills by the token, and dispatch volumes turn tokens into real money fast. A company can buy a dispatch demo with animated vans gliding across a map, or build the unglamorous foundation: a knowledge base of technician skills, labeled job outcomes, and parts-inventory truth per van. Since rankings anchor this series, here is my openly editorial ranking of 5 home-services AI misses, judged by one criterion: how far each miss sits from the van, the wrench, and the customer.

  1. AI dispatch that ignores technician skill nuance. This tops my list because it optimizes drive time and destroys fix rates. Sending the nearest tech instead of the right tech converts one visit into two, and the customer pays for the lesson.
  2. Predictive arrival times quoted to the minute. Traffic, parking, and the previous job's surprises make minute-level precision fiction. Alim notes that a false precise ETA damages trust more than an honest wide window.
  3. AI upsell scripts pushed to technicians. The model suggests a water-softener pitch while the tech is under a sink; the tech reads the room and ignores it. Unused features are just invoice decorations.
  4. Sentiment analysis on customer calls. It flags frustration after the damage is done and explains nothing about the cause. An engineer would rather log the labeled outcome and fix the routing.
  5. AI-generated job summaries for invoicing. This ranks last only because the damage is clerical. The summaries hallucinate parts and hours with total confidence, and someone still has to check every line before the bill goes out.

None of this argues against technology in home services. It argues for knowledge captured by someone who respects the wrench. The firms getting real value are the ones where an engineer rode along in the van, learned why the dispatcher keeps a paper list despite the software, and built the skills database before the routing model.
