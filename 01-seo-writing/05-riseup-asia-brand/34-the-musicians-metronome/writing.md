# The Musician's Metronome

The Melbourne street musician had set up under the awning of a closed bank, out of the drizzle. A guitar case lay open on the wet Melbourne pavement with a few coins in it, and clipped to the music stand was a metronome: a battered wooden pyramid with a swinging arm, its paint worn off at the corners. It ticked at an unhurried tempo while something slow and minor filled the Melbourne air. Oddly, the playing did not follow it. The music moved around the beat, ahead of it, behind it, the way a good conversationalist circles a point without landing on it. Meanwhile, the metronome kept perfect time; the Melbourne music kept perfect feeling. They were related, and they were not the same thing.

That distinction has been on my mind while watching the music industry's AI gold rush. Labels and platforms are pouring money into generative music, AI A&R, and synthetic artists. The supply numbers are staggering. [Deezer's detection tool tagged over 13.4 million fully AI-generated tracks in 2025, and AI uploads now make up 44% of the platform's daily uploads, yet account for only 1 to 3% of streams](https://sqmagazine.co.uk/ai-music-statistics/). Suno, the leading generative music service, [raised a $250 million round, reached a $2.45 billion valuation, and struck the first licensing deal with Warner Music Group](https://musically.com/2025/11/25/ai-music-firm-suno-strikes-first-licensing-deal-with-warner-music-group/), with more than 100 million people having tried it. Meanwhile, the machines make music at industrial scale, while the Melbourne listeners, so far, mostly listen to something else.

Since rankings anchor this series, here is my openly editorial ranking of 5 Melbourne music AI myths, judged by 1 criterion: how completely each myth confuses making noise with making music.

1. AI A&R will find the next hit. This tops my list because hits are outliers by definition, and models trained on the past are allergic to outliers. The demo scores last year's Melbourne hits perfectly and this year's hits not at all.
2. Generative music democratizes creation. 100 million creators generating tracks nobody streams is not democratization; it is a warehouse. Alim notes that supply without curation is just storage with better marketing.
3. AI mastering replaces the mastering engineer. It replaces the technical part and misses the taste part, which was the actual job. Loudness is math; the final 5% is judgment.
4. Synthetic artists are the future of labels. They are the future of content farms. A Melbourne fan base is a relationship, and relationships with press releases do not fill venues.
5. AI-generated liner notes and bios enrich the catalog. This ranks last only because the damage is comic. The systems invent band histories with total confidence, and somewhere a Melbourne folk singer from nowhere now officially formed in 1974.
