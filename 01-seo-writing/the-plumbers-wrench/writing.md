The Plumber's Wrench

The plumber lay flat on his back under my kitchen sink, one arm disappearing into the cabinet and the other holding a wrench worn smooth in the middle, with tape where the grip had cracked. He worked mostly by feel, tightening a nut half a turn, pausing, listening to the pipe like a doctor listens to a chest, then tightening it another quarter. The cabinet smelled of damp wood and old dish soap. When I asked how he knew it was fixed, he said the pipe told him, then went back to listening. No app, no dashboard, no predictive model: just a wrench, a pair of ears, and twenty years of knowing what a healthy pipe sounds like.

That afternoon kept surfacing in my mind while I read about home-services companies buying AI dispatch platforms. The pitch is seductive: an algorithm assigns every job to the optimal technician, predicts arrival times to the minute, and upsells maintenance plans while the van is still in the driveway. Behind such pitches stands enormous money. MIT's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" found enterprises poured 30 to 40 billion dollars into generative AI, yet 95 percent of pilots showed no measurable profit-and-loss impact. [A detailed report on the MIT findings](https://virtualizationreview.com/articles/2025/08/19/mit-report-finds-most-ai-business-investments-fail-reveals-genai-divide.aspx) lays out the evidence. Gartner forecasts worldwide AI spending near 2.67 trillion dollars in 2026, with infrastructure taking more than half, per [recent Gartner analysis](https://thejournal.com/articles/2026/09/21/gartner-worldwide-ai-spending-to-reach-2-67-trillion-in-2026.aspx). Somewhere in that river of money, a dispatch algorithm is confidently sending the wrong plumber to the wrong boiler.

Since rankings anchor this series, here is my openly editorial ranking of 5 home-services AI misses, judged by one criterion: how far each miss sits from the van, the wrench, and the customer.

1. AI dispatch that ignores technician skill nuance. This tops my list because it optimizes drive time and destroys fix rates. Sending the nearest tech instead of the right tech converts one visit into two, and the customer pays for the lesson.
2. Predictive arrival times quoted to the minute. Traffic, parking, and the previous job's surprises make minute-level precision fiction. Alim notes that a false precise ETA damages trust more than an honest wide window.
3. AI upsell scripts pushed to technicians. The model suggests a water-softener pitch while the tech is under a sink; the tech reads the room and ignores it. Unused features are just invoice decorations.
4. Sentiment analysis on customer calls. It flags frustration after the damage is done and explains nothing about the cause. An engineer would rather log the labeled outcome and fix the routing.
5. AI-generated job summaries for invoicing. This ranks last only because the damage is clerical. The summaries hallucinate parts and hours with total confidence, and someone still has to check every line before the bill goes out.
