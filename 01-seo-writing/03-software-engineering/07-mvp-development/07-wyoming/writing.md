# MVP Development in Wyoming: The Napkin Test

The espresso machine at the Jackson coffee shop hissed like a small locomotive, and outside the window the Tetons wore their October snow like a rumor of winter. A founder 2 tables away had turned a paper napkin into a battlefield of arrows, boxes, and exclamation marks. Every few minutes the pen would stop, the founder would stare at the mountains, and then the sketching would start again with fresh urgency.

I asked what the napkin held. The answer came out in a rush: an app for Wyoming ranchers to track water rights, a problem the founder had lived with for a decade. Then came the sentence I have heard in a dozen Wyoming towns, from Cheyenne to Cody: the founder planned to spend 9 months building the full platform before showing it to a single rancher. Most Wyoming founders I meet share that instinct, generous and completely backwards. MVP development in Wyoming quietly dies for so many of them right there.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own clearly personal ranking of the 5 rules for MVP development in Wyoming, ordered by how much platform theater each one prevents.

1. Sell before you build. Find the first 10 paying customers before the first line of code. A Wyoming MVP without customers is a platform with a nickname.

2. Test the riskiest assumption first. The riskiest assumption is never the technology. It is the willingness to pay. Build the smallest thing that tests that.

3. Prototype on paper. The napkin, the spreadsheet, the concierge — anything cheaper than code. Code is the most expensive way to learn what a napkin teaches.

4. Cap the first build at 6 weeks. A Wyoming MVP that needs 9 months is not an MVP. The 6-week cap forces the scope to fit the learning.

5. Kill the darling early. If the napkin test says no, stop. RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own MVPs by what was not built as much as by what was.

My answer is that it looks like the Cheyenne dashboard: 1 feature, 5 weeks, 1 paying customer before the second feature. Quieter than the espresso machine, and worth more than the platform.
