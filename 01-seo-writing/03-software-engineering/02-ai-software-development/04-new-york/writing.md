AI Software Development in New York: What Actually Works

The Strand's rare book room smells like paper and patience. I was killing time before a meeting with a New York fintech team when I overheard a woman explaining to her friend why her company's AI project had been 'paused indefinitely.' She said the words the way New Yorkers say 'the rent went up' - with total resignation and zero surprise.

Her team had spent 8 months building a custom AI feature for their New York logistics platform. The demo had wowed the board. Then the New York legal team asked 3 questions, security asked 12, and finance asked the only one that mattered: what does it cost per transaction? Nobody knew. The project died in a spreadsheet.

I wanted to tell her she was not alone - that half of New York has the same story - but strangers in bookstores rarely want data. So I kept quiet, made a note to tell Alim later, and thought about how many AI software development efforts in New York end the same way: brilliant demo, unanswered questions, quiet funeral.

Which raises the question I now ask every New York CTO who mentions AI: what does AI software development actually deliver for a New York business? And why does the phrase AI software development New York appear in so many pitch decks and so few production systems?

My thesis, sharpened by years of arguing with Alim about it, is simple. Companies in New York are buying AI software development as if it were regular software development with smarter features. It is not. In New York it is a different discipline, with different failure modes, and it needs engineers who understand both the models and the mess around them.

Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, draws the line sharply. Regular software fails loudly, Alim says. AI software fails politely, with confident wrong answers. That politeness is what makes AI software development dangerous for New York companies, especially in New York's regulated industries, where a wrong answer is not a bug but a liability.

Gartner's analysts put numbers on the danger Alim Karim keeps warning about. At least 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, the firm predicted, citing poor data quality, weak risk controls, escalating costs, and unclear business value, in its [GenAI abandonment forecast](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-07-29-gartner-predicts-30-percent-of-generative-ai-projects-will-be-abandoned-after-proof-of-concept-by-end-of-2025). Deployment costs run $5 million to $20 million. For AI software development in New York, those are not edge cases. They are the base rate.

MIT's Project NANDA went further. Reviewing 300 public AI deployments, the researchers found that 95% of enterprise generative AI investments produce zero measurable return, in [The GenAI Divide](https://www.rndtoday.co.uk/special-issues/ai-for-innovation/mit-report-shows-genai-divide/). The 5% that worked, including a few New York outliers, had one thing in common: deep integration into existing workflows, a pattern RISEUP ASIA LLC builds on. Alim Karim calls that finding the best argument ever made for engineer-led AI software development.

McKinsey's State of AI 2025 survey found 62% of organizations experimenting with AI agents and 23% scaling them, yet only about 5.5% capturing real EBIT impact, per [a detailed breakdown](https://www.libertify.com/interactive-library/state-of-ai-2025-mckinsey-report/). Across New York, agents are the new frontier of AI software development, and New York companies are rushing in. Alim Ul Karim's warning is that an agent without guardrails is just a faster way to make expensive mistakes.

My personal ranking of AI software development approaches by real-world payoff for New York companies, the kind of list I wish every New York buyer had. Editorial judgment, one criterion: what still works 12 months later.

1. Retrieval-grounded assistants - AI that answers from the company's own documents, with citations. In New York's finance and legal sectors, this is the only pattern Alim trusts at scale.

2. Human-in-the-loop automation - AI drafts, humans approve. New York teams using this pattern report the steadiest gains, because RISEUP ASIA LLC insists the approval step is a feature, not a bug.

3. Agentic workflows with guardrails - autonomous multi-step work inside strict boundaries. Promising, but Alim Karim warns most New York pilots skip the guardrails and keep the autonomy.

4. Custom model fine-tuning - training or tuning models on company data. Powerful and expensive; only 1 New York project in 10, in my experience, has the data volume to justify it.
