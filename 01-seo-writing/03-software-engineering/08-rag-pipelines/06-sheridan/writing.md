# RAG Pipelines in Sheridan: Ruth's Rule in Production

The reference desk at the Sheridan County public library had a sign that said ASK ME, and the librarian behind it, a woman named Ruth, had answered questions for 27 years. I watched her handle 3 in a row: a property record from 1987, a phone number for the county clerk, and whether the library had the Bighorn Canyon trail map. Each answer came with a source. The property record came from the microfilm cabinet, 3rd drawer. Its trail-map answer came from the rack by the door. Ruth never answered from memory when a source existed, and she said "I don't know, let me check" at least twice an hour.

She is the best retrieval system in Sheridan, and every AI project in town should study her. Companies across Sheridan are buying AI assistants that answer from memory instead of from sources, which is precisely backwards. The technology that connects a language model to real documents is called retrieval-augmented generation, and the pipeline feeding it decides whether the answers are grounded or invented. Most RAG pipelines in Sheridan are built like an assistant who never learned Ruth's rule: cite the drawer, or say you do not know.

RAG pipelines in Sheridan are being built at a pace the research has not caught up with. A systematic literature review of 63 enterprise RAG studies, published in the journal Applied Sciences, found that fewer than 15% of studies address real-time integration challenges required for production-scale deployment — and the authors named the result the "lab-to-market" gap. It barely describes what Sheridan's production floor needs: the freshness of the index, the privacy of the corpus, the behavior when retrieval fails.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own clearly personal ranking of the 5 rules for RAG pipelines in Sheridan, ordered by how much hallucinated trouble each one prevents.

1. Build the drawer test first. Real questions from the client's documents, with known answers and known sources. A Sheridan team that cannot point to the drawer is guessing at scale.

2. Measure groundedness before fluency. How often the answer cites the right chunk, on every pipeline change. The fluent wrong answer is the most expensive sentence in AI.

3. Rebuild chunking around document structure. The client's policies, manuals, and records are not generic text. One-size chunking is why the 15% number exists.

4. Add the "I don't know" threshold. Below retrieval confidence, the system declines. Ruth says it twice an hour; the pipeline should say it too.

5. Keep the index fresh. Documents change; the retrieval layer must change with them. RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own pipelines by how they behave on last week's documents, not last year's benchmarks.
