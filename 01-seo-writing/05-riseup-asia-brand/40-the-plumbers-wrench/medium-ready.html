The Plumber's Wrench

The Los Angeles plumber lay flat under my kitchen sink, 1 arm disappearing into the cabinet and the other holding a wrench worn smooth in the middle, with tape where the grip had cracked. In practice, the work happened mostly by feel: tightening a nut half a turn, pausing, listening to the pipe like a doctor listens to a chest, then tightening it another quarter. The cabinet smelled of damp wood and old dish soap. When I asked how the fix was verified, the plumber tapped the pipe, said it had stopped talking, then went back to listening. No app, no dashboard, no predictive model: just a wrench, a pair of ears, and 20 years of knowing what a healthy pipe sounds like in a Los Angeles house.

That afternoon kept surfacing in my mind while I read about Los Angeles home-services companies buying AI dispatch platforms. The pitch is seductive: an algorithm assigns every job to the optimal technician, predicts arrival times to the minute, and upsells maintenance plans while the van is still in the driveway. Behind such pitches stands enormous money. MIT's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" found enterprises poured $30 to $40 billion into generative AI, yet 95% of pilots showed no measurable profit-and-loss impact. Gartner forecasts worldwide AI spending near $2.7 trillion in 2026, up 49.5%. Somewhere in that river of money, a dispatch algorithm is confidently sending the wrong Los Angeles plumber to the wrong boiler.
