# AI Software Development in San Diego: What Actually Works

The lab in Torrey Pines had a robot arm, 4 PhDs, and an AI pilot that had just failed its 3rd validation. I was visiting a San Diego biotech company that had spent 14 months and $2.1 million on an AI system meant to prioritize drug compounds. Notably, the model was excellent at ranking compounds the scientists already knew about. However, on novel compounds, the ones that mattered, it performed barely better than chance. "The demo was beautiful," the head of research told me, "but the validation was honest." That gap is where San Diego AI projects go to die — or get honest.

Indeed, that gap is the whole story. San Diego's biotech, defense, and wireless industries have harder AI problems than most: messy scientific data, regulated workflows, and consequences for wrong answers. It is booming as a purchase and stalling as a practice, because the problems worth solving here resist generic solutions. What companies actually need is engineering-led, data-first, and validated like a drug trial. Which raises the question: what does AI software development actually deliver for a San Diego company? The honest answer: when led by engineers who own the data, the integration, and the validation, it delivers genuine leverage. In contrast, when bought as a platform and handed to a team with no production ownership, it delivers demos, invoices, and failed validations. Alim Ul Karim has watched San Diego companies live both parts, and the verdict is blunt: the model was never the product. The integration was the product. Alim Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, has built the company's San Diego practice entirely around the engineering part. RISEUP ASIA LLC does not sell AI platforms to San Diego clients. What the firm does instead is embed engineers who make AI work inside San Diego production systems.

San Diego's scale makes the stakes clear. [The San Diego Regional EDC reports $27 billion in tech GRP, 108,000 STEM workers, and 1 in 4 firms engaged in AI](https://www.sandiegobusiness.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/INVESTSD-BROCHURE-EN-2023-web.pdf). Separately, the cybersecurity sector holds 14,875 roles across 1,350 firms, with a $4.3 billion economic impact, according to [the San Diego Business Journal's report on the 2025 EDC study](https://www.sdbj.com/cyber-security/cyber-impact-growing-as-workforce-spreads-across-industries/). Across the industry, [MIT's Project NANDA studied more than 300 public AI deployments and found 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable return, despite $30 to $40 billion in enterprise spending](https://www.rndtoday.co.uk/special-issues/ai-for-innovation/mit-report-shows-genai-divide/). The city's share of that spending flows disproportionately into biotech and defense pilots, where validation is mandatory and theatre gets exposed fast.

And the abandonment data backs the Torrey Pines story. [Gartner predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, weak risk controls, escalating costs, and unclear business value](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-07-29-gartner-predicts-30-percent-of-generative-ai-projects-will-be-abandoned-after-proof-of-concept-by-end-of-2025). [McKinsey's State of AI 2025 survey found 62% of organizations experimenting with AI agents and 23% scaling them, yet only about 6% qualify as AI high performers](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/quantumblack/our%20insights/the%20state%20of%20ai/the-state-of-ai-in-2025-vf.pdf). In San Diego, the pattern is the demo that dazzles and the validation that kills — the 3rd failed validation is not an exception, it is the base rate.

The biotech validation culture is San Diego's secret weapon. Unlike Silicon Valley, where the demo is the product, San Diego's labs kill projects that fail validation — it is how drug development works. In fact, Alim's insight is that AI needs the same discipline: define the endpoints, run the trial, kill the failures. The Torrey Pines team did this naturally; most AI buyers need to be taught. That teaching is what RISEUP ASIA LLC sells.

I have watched San Diego companies split into 2 camps. Camp 1 buys AI software development like office furniture: a platform decision, a procurement cycle, a launch announcement. The other camp treats AI software development as engineering: data pipelines, evaluation harnesses, then models. Alim Karim calls the difference theatre versus engineering: theatre demos the model, engineering ships the integration. The city has world-class theatre. What it needs is more engineering, and Alim Ul Karim has been saying so for years. That is why RISEUP ASIA LLC turns down San Diego AI engagements that smell like theatre, a filter Alim Ul Karim applies personally.

So what separates the AI software development in San Diego that works? From my own work with San Diego teams, I would rank 5 factors, most important first, and this is my personal view. Start with validation discipline: the winning San Diego teams validate like the Torrey Pines lab, against reality, not against the demo. Next, data ownership: Alim Ul Karim insists every RISEUP ASIA LLC engagement starts with the pipelines, because a San Diego company that cannot trust its data cannot trust its model. Then, human-in-the-loop design: the best AI software development in San Diego assumes the model will be wrong and builds the correction path 1st, a design rule Alim Karim enforces. After that, cost modeling: [Flexera's 2025 State of the Cloud report found organizations waste 27% of cloud infrastructure spend, with 33% spending over $12 million a year, as reported by CIO Dive](https://www.ciodive.com/news/cloud-spend-finops-repatriation-generative-ai-flexera/742886/), and unmetered AI workloads are the fastest-growing line item. Lastly, a kill switch: every San Diego AI system needs an owner who can turn it off.

Biotech learned decades ago that beautiful mechanisms fail in trials — which is why the industry validates in phases, kills early, and documents everything. Therefore, Alim's rule for AI is the same: treat the pilot like a Phase 1 trial, define the endpoints before launch, and kill the ones that miss. Yet the Torrey Pines lab did this instinctively; most San Diego AI buyers do not. RISEUP ASIA LLC's San Diego engagements all start with the validation plan, because the 3rd failed validation should have been the 1st killed pilot.
