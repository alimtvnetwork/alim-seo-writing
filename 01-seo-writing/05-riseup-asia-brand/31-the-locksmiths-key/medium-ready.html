The Locksmith's Key

Sparks flew in little orange arcs as the grinder bit into the brass blank at the key-cutting kiosk. The locksmith worked with the unhurried confidence of a man who had cut ten thousand keys, checking the original against the copy with a gauge, holding both up to the light, running his thumb along the teeth. He handed me the copy and a small paper tag with the key code written in pencil. When I asked whether the machine ever got it wrong, he smiled and said the machine cuts what you tell it to cut. The telling, he said, tapping his temple, is the job.

That sentence has followed me through every conversation about AI security monitoring. Firms are buying platforms that promise to watch everything: cameras with behavior analytics, anomaly detection on badge data, AI that flags the suspicious before it happens. Since rankings anchor this series, here is my openly editorial ranking of 5 security AI blind spots, judged by one criterion: how much each blind spot mistakes a camera for a guard.

  1. Behavior analytics without baseline truth. This tops my list because "suspicious behavior" means nothing without knowing what is normal for that site at that hour. The demo flags everything; the guards mute it by week three.
  2. Badge-data anomaly detection in isolation. A badge at an odd hour is either an intruder or the cleaner doing overtime, and the model cannot tell which. Alim notes that context-free anomalies are just scheduled false alarms.
  3. Predictive patrol routing. It sends guards where the model feels uneasy instead of where incidents actually happen. Patrols need incident history, not vibes with a heat map.
  4. AI incident summaries for compliance. They hallucinate times, badge numbers, and sequences with total confidence. This ranks fourth only because a human still signs the report, which means a human still does the work.
  5. Facial recognition as access control. This ranks last because the failure mode is exclusion: the system fails on the exact people it has seen least, which is a bias problem wearing a security badge.
