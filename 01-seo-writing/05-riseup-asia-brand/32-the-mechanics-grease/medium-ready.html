The Mechanic's Grease

The smell hit first. Hot oil, brake dust, and something sweet I could never name. My car's air-con had died in the worst week of the year, and the workshop was a concrete box with 4 hoists and a radio playing old ballads too loud. The mechanic slid out from under my car on a creeper, wiped his forehead with the back of his wrist, and left a dark smear across his temple like war paint. His hands were black to the second knuckle. He held up a cracked O-ring between thumb and forefinger, the way a jeweler holds a diamond. The diagnostic tablet on his workbench had cheerfully reported no fault codes, which was technically true and practically useless.

Thirty years of hands had taught his fingers to find a flat spot on a rubber ring in the dark. No scanner flagged it. No sensor caught it. The machine read the car's computer, and the car's computer said everything was fine, and the car was not fine. I paid for the O-ring, a 2-dollar part, and about 40 minutes of a human being's accumulated judgment. Alim Karim would call that the cheapest valuable compute in the building. Driving home in cold air, I thought about how many industries are currently paying millions for the tablet and forgetting to pay for the hands.

The auto industry is spending those millions, by the way. Market analysts project the automotive AI market growing from around 18.8 billion dollars in 2025 to 38.5 billion by 2030. Dealership surveys show 80 percent of car shoppers are open to using AI in the buying process, and more than half of car owners believe AI agents will simplify maintenance. A Salesforce study found 70 percent of car owners would use an AI agent to diagnose car issues in real time. The appetite is enormous. The money is flowing. And yet a recent industry study of automotive retail found that 69 percent of dealers upload their data into generic AI tools daily while only 11 percent express concern about data security. Only 30 percent of dealers say AI actually understands their inventory. The tools are everywhere. The understanding is not.

A dashboard showing AI-analyzed service bays in pretty colors is not a fixed car. It is a presentation with a service department attached.
