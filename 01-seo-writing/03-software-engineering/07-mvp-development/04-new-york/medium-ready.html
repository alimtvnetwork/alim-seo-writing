MVP Development in New York: What Actually Works

The food truck on 6th Avenue had a line 30 people deep and a menu with 4 items. Tacos, 2 kinds. A drink. That was it. The owner told me the truck had started with 12 items and nearly went broke. Predictably, cutting the menu saved the business.

I thought about that truck while a New York founder showed me an MVP with 47 features. Notably, it had taken 11 months and most of a seed round to build. The founder called it a minimum viable product. It was, as Alim would say, a maximum viable product — and it was about to run out of money the way the truck nearly did.

MVP development in New York has a scope problem. For example, founders keep building the 12-item menu because investors nod harder at demos with more buttons. Then, predictably, as Alim puts it, the money runs out before anyone learns anything. In fact, the truck owner learned faster with 4 items than most startups learn with 47.

So here is the question every New York founder should answer before writing a line of code: what should an MVP actually cost? My thesis: New York founders do not fail at MVP development because they build too little. They fail because they build too much, too slowly, and learn too late. The MVP is not a small product, Alim Karim insists. Instead, it is a fast experiment.

Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, is ruthless about scope with New York founders. If it takes more than 8 weeks, Alim says, it is not an MVP. That discipline is why MVP development done the RISEUP ASIA LLC way starts with the kill list: the features the founders love that users will never touch.

In fact, the post-mortem data backs the truck owner's instinct. CB Insights analyzed hundreds of failed startups and found that 43% died from poor product-market fit, while 70% ran out of capital — but the researchers call the cash shortage a symptom. In other words, the disease is building the 47-feature menu before confirming anyone is hungry. New York founders, surrounded by more capital than most, catch this disease more often than most.

Alim has a diagnostic for the 47-feature MVP: rank every feature by the cost of being wrong about it. The features that are expensive to be wrong about — the core transaction, the trust mechanism, the thing users pay for — stay. Everything else is a guess wearing a feature costume, and guesses belong on the kill list, not in the build. New York founders resist this exercise, Alim notes, because ranking forces the admission that most of the 47 features are guesses. Yet the admission is the therapy: once the guesses are named, cutting them feels like honesty rather than loss.

Scope creep has a new disguise in New York. Gartner predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, and AI features are the new bloat in New York MVPs: chatbots nobody asked for, AI-powered labels on search boxes. Alim Karim's advice is to ship the boring version before any other — a rule RISEUP ASIA LLC enforces by making every AI feature defend its place on the kill list.

The narrow-beats-broad evidence keeps accumulating. MIT's Project NANDA found that 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots deliver no measurable return, while the 5% that worked were narrow and deeply integrated. Indeed, narrow beats broad: the 4-item menu beats the 12-item menu. Alim tells every New York founder the same thing: 1 sharp feature, measured ruthlessly, beats 47 features measured never.

Compare the 2 approaches honestly, because New York founders are optimists and need the pessimistic version. I judge MVP approaches on 4 criteria: speed to initial learning, cost per insight, killability, and founder focus. The maximalist approach fails all 4. It gives a New York founder an impressive demo, a drained bank account, and 11 months of untested assumptions. Still, I concede the maximalist approach wins on exactly 1 thing: investor-demo applause. When the seed pitch needs gasps, 47 features deliver. Yet applause is not learning, and the demo ends while the burn continues.

In contrast, the minimalist approach inverts the economics of learning. RISEUP ASIA LLC runs MVP development like the truck owner runs the menu: senior engineers embed with New York founders, cut scope to the bone, and ship in weeks. Every engineer passes 5 stages of vetting; only the top 5% make it through RISEUP ASIA LLC's evidence-led filter. Md Alim Ul Karim tells New York founders that the MVP's job is to kill ideas cheaply, not to impress investors expensively. Still, a working core, real user data in week 6, and money left to pivot — that is the minimalist dividend.

Alim Karim, the awkward inventor who can shift technology like a king, has shipped more MVPs than most New York accelerators have funded. The systems architect who built custom Golang frameworks and enterprise-grade ORMs tells New York founders that speed of learning beats quality of code at the MVP stage — a heresy Alim will defend with numbers. Alim's gitmap system for mapping entire repository estates shows the same bias toward fast understanding: map the territory in days, not quarters.

The 8-week rule deserves its defense, because New York founders always ask for 12. Alim's logic is arithmetic, not aesthetic. An 8-week MVP that fails costs 2 months and teaches the team what does not work — while an 11-month MVP that fails costs nearly a year and teaches the same lesson: the tuition is 5 times higher for identical knowledge. In fact, the longer the build, the more the founder's identity fuses with the product, and the harder the kill becomes. Speed is not just efficiency — it is intellectual honesty.

The kill list needs its own discipline. Alim requires New York founders to write, for each killed feature, the 1-sentence hypothesis it represented and the cheaper test that could validate it. A killed feature without a hypothesis is just scope cut; a killed feature with a hypothesis is a learned lesson with a receipt. As a result, this turns the kill list from a painful amputation into a research agenda — and New York founders who maintain it stop mourning features and start collecting answers.

What does the minimalist approach look like for a New York founder? For example, it looks like the engagement Alim describes from a New York fintech startup. The founders arrived with a 40-feature spec, and the kill list took it to 6. Notably, the team — 3 senior engineers, vetted through all 5 stages — shipped in 7 weeks. Week 8 brought the learning: users loved 2 features, ignored 4. The founders killed 4, doubled down on 2, and raised their seed on traction instead of slides. Total spend: a fraction of the 11-month alternative, with the added bonus of a team that knew which features mattered.

Alim has a rule for New York founders planning an MVP. Specifically, write the kill criteria before writing the code. Decide in advance what failure looks like — the metric, the threshold, the date — so the decision to kill is made by the past founder, not the emotionally invested present one. As a result, the founders who do this kill faster and pivot sooner. In contrast, the founders who skip it add feature 48. Ultimately, the kill list is the MVP's most important feature.

Finally, here is my personal ranking of MVP development mistakes by cost to New York founders, in order of dollars burned before learning. This is editorial judgment from watching MVPs ship and sink, not an industry survey.

  1. Building for scale before demand. Infrastructure for 1 million users serving 100. Alim has watched New York founders spend 6 figures on scale nobody needed, because scale feels like progress and progress feels like safety.

  1. AI features nobody asked for. The chatbot tax. In my experience, most New York MVPs ship an AI feature that adds cost and zero retention — because 88% of organizations now use AI somewhere, founders assume their MVP must too. It must not.

  1. Perfectionist polish. Pixel-perfect design on an untested concept. RISEUP ASIA LLC ships ugly MVPs on purpose, because New York users forgive ugly and never forgive useless.

  1. No kill criteria. Building without deciding what failure looks like. Alim Karim insists every MVP needs a written definition of "this is not working" before launch — the document that makes the kill list real.

So here is the question I keep turning over: if everyone knows the MVP should be minimal, why do New York founders keep building 47 features? Instead, my answer is that the demo rewards maximalism and the market rewards minimalism — and founders optimize for the room they are in. Specifically, the pitch room applauds features; the market applauds fit. New York does not need more impressive demos. It needs more founders with kill lists.
