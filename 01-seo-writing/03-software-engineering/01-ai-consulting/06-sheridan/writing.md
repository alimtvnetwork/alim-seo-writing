# AI Consulting in Sheridan: What Actually Works

The hotel lobby in Sheridan had the particular quiet of a weekday morning: leather chairs, a fireplace with no fire, and a consultant at the corner table rehearsing a slide deck out loud. I caught fragments while waiting in that Sheridan lobby: AI readiness assessment, maturity model, phase-gated roadmap. The deck was 60 slides. Slide 1 promised transformation. Somewhere around slide 40, I suspected, came the part where the Sheridan client paid for the assessment of the assessment.

I have watched this ritual in a dozen towns, and Alim Karim has watched it in more. It arrives in Sheridan with impressive vocabulary and leaves with a PDF. For example, the Sheridan executive gets a heat map, a maturity score, and a recommendation to buy the consultant's implementation services. In the end, nothing has shipped, the heat map has cooled, and the only intelligence that was artificial was the promise. The consulting was real, the invoice was real, and everything else was theatre.

What does AI consulting look like when it actually leads to working software? Therefore, that is the question Sheridan buyers should ask before they sign, because the default answer in this industry is a document, not a deployment. Alim Ul Karim answers with unusual generosity toward consultants. Indeed, good consulting exists, says Alim Karim, even in Sheridan; it just rarely looks like a deck. Real advisory work ends with a system in production and a client team that can run it. Everything else is research the client funded for the consultant's benefit. The test Alim proposes for Sheridan buyers is disarmingly simple: ask what the consultant will have shipped, not what they will have said, by week 8.

Meanwhile, behind the ritual sits staggering money. The global AI consulting services market is projected to grow from $10.12 billion to $82.35 billion, according to [Value Market Research](https://www.valuemarketresearch.com/report/ai-consulting-services-market). As it happens, consultants take their cut first in every Sheridan boardroom, because advice scales better than engineering. In fact, Sheridan companies are buying this advice in growing numbers. Predictably, when a market grows 8-fold, the number of sellers grows faster than the number of sellers who can ship — and Sheridan, a town that measures people by whether they show up when the work is hard, is about to meet a lot of sellers. The questions in this article are Sheridan's armor against that army.

Notably, the industry's own data explains why the deck keeps winning and the software keeps losing. MIT's Project NANDA studied more than 300 public AI deployments and found 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable return, according to [a 2025 report on the state of AI in business](https://www.rndtoday.co.uk/special-issues/ai-for-innovation/mit-report-shows-genai-divide/). The result is a consulting story: the pilots were advised, assessed, and roadmapped, and 95% of them never paid back. Meanwhile, the maturity model was delivered; the production system was not. Honestly, demos are cheap and production is expensive, says Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur. Alim Karim calls deck-first consulting the most expensive storytelling in business, because the story is always about transformation and the invoice is always real.

In fact, McKinsey's 2025 State of AI survey found 88% of companies now use AI in at least 1 business function, yet 62% are merely experimenting with AI agents while only 23% are scaling them, according to [McKinsey's primary report](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/quantumblack/our%20insights/the%20state%20of%20ai/the-state-of-ai-in-2025-vf.pdf). That is why the gap between experimenting and scaling is where consultants should earn their fees — and where the deck-first firms vanish. Experimentation is easy to advise from a hotel lobby. Instead, scaling requires someone inside the repository, inside the data, inside the pager rotation. Gartner predicts 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, according to [Gartner's own press release](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-07-29-gartner-predicts-30-percent-of-generative-ai-projects-will-be-abandoned-after-proof-of-concept-by-end-of-2025). Consequently, every abandoned proof of concept had a consultant who billed for the recommendation and was gone before the result.

Set the 2 approaches side by side and the contrast stops being subtle. Deck-first consulting gives a Sheridan company 60 slides, a maturity score, and a recommendation to spend more. By contrast, builder-led advising gives the same Sheridan company a working system, a trained team, and an advisor whose reputation depends on the deploy. Compare them on honest criteria: what is the deliverable, who carries the production pager, and what does the client own when the engagement ends. Alim Karim's test is the pager question: if it breaks at 2 AM, who gets called — and do they answer? 1 sells the map. The other walks the territory. Here is the honest concession: the assessment-first firm is not always wrong. A Sheridan company that genuinely does not know where AI fits its business can use 2 weeks of honest exploration. However, exploration is a 2-week engagement with a named decision at the end, not a 60-slide phase 1 with a phase 2 that has no date.

The economics favor the builder in a way the deck can never match. A 60-slide assessment costs a Sheridan company 6 figures and teaches the consultant about the business. By contrast, a 6-week embedded engagement costs less and leaves the business with a working system and a trained team. Sheridan's no-nonsense character makes it a hard town to fool twice. I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own editorial ranking of consultant tests, and the scoring criterion is the 1 Alim suggested to me in that quiet lobby: which questions expose theatre before the contract is signed. Here are 4 questions every Sheridan buyer should ask, most revealing first. 1st: "What will you have shipped by week 8?" Not assessed, not mapped — shipped, running in production. In short, a consultant who cannot name a week-8 deliverable is selling process, and Sheridan should not buy process. 2nd: "Who on your team has carried a 2 AM page?" Names, systems, war stories with specifics. Advice about production from people who have never been paged is fiction with an invoice. 3rd: "What happens to our team when you leave?" Training, handover, documentation, the plan for independence. RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own advisors by whether the Sheridan client still thrives 6 months later. 4th: "Show me something you killed." A recommendation the consultant argued against, a project stopped early, a client talked out of spending. Frankly, advisors who never say no are salespeople, and Sheridan can spot the difference.

Ask those 4 questions and watch what happens. The deck-first consultant will answer question 1 with a methodology. Meanwhile, the builder will answer with a date. That is the entire test. Methodologies are what consultants sell when they cannot sell results, and Sheridan buyers have learned to hear the difference. I have seen 1 executive end a pitch 10 minutes in with question 2, and I have seen another hire on the spot after question 3 revealed a real handover plan. The questions do not just filter consultants. They reveal whether the buyer is ready to demand shipping.
