# Dedicated Development Teams in Los Angeles: What Actually Works

The machine shop in El Segundo smelled like cutting oil and ambition. I was touring a supplier that makes components for Los Angeles aerospace companies, and the operations director showed me the software tracking the floor: a 15-year-old system held together by 1 retiring engineer and a lot of prayer. "We have developers," she said, gesturing at an org chart. "What we don't have is a team that owns this." In Los Angeles, that distinction is worth millions.

Notice was already on the retiring engineer's desk. Prayer was doing most of the work.

Personally, I left El Segundo thinking about the difference between developers and a team, because the dedicated development teams Los Angeles companies hire usually arrive as the 1st and never become the 2nd. A vendor's bench, billed by the hour, is not a team. What Los Angeles companies actually need is a small senior unit that owns outcomes like an in-house team, without the 6-month hiring cycle. Instead, those are 2 different products wearing the same name, and only 1 of them fixes the factory floor. It is the definition Alim Karim uses at RISEUP ASIA LLC.

Meanwhile, follow the Los Angeles dollars and the buying makes sense. The custom software development market was valued at $44.2 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $213.4 billion by 2035, according to [Global Market Insights' industry analysis](https://www.gminsights.com/industry-analysis/custom-software-development-market). Los Angeles sits inside the region spending the largest share of it: studios rebuilding post-production pipelines, aerospace suppliers modernizing floor systems, logistics firms rewiring the ports. Consequently, every 1 of those operations needs software that a bench of rented juniors cannot build, and Los Angeles buyers keep paying senior-team prices for junior-bench results.

The mismatch is visible in every Los Angeles renewal conversation. Meanwhile, the invoice claims a senior team; the standup shows juniors waiting for instructions. The gap between those 2 realities is where the money goes — not into software, but into supervision, rework, and the slow discovery that the vendor's bench cannot read the codebase it was hired to modernize. Buying teams is expensive. Renting benches is more expensive, because you pay twice: once for the hours, once for the rewrite.

Here is the question Los Angeles CTOs should ask every vendor that pitches a dedicated team: who owns the outcome when the system breaks at 2 AM? If the answer is a ticket queue, you are buying staff, not a team. By contrast, when the answer is a named human who knows your codebase, you are buying the real thing. Alim Ul Karim, a veteran software architect from Kuala Lumpur, tells Los Angeles prospects that a team without a named owner is just a group chat with invoices. The line gets laughs in Los Angeles boardrooms, and then it gets written into the contract. Then the laughter stops, because the contract now names a human.

This is where my thesis gets uncomfortable for the AI-first crowd. For example, Los Angeles companies are layering AI coding tools over junior vendor benches and calling the result a modern development team. The arithmetic does not hold. MIT's Project NANDA reviewed more than 300 public AI deployments and found 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable return, with only 5% reaching production with real value, according to [a 2025 report on the state of AI in business](https://www.rndtoday.co.uk/special-issues/ai-for-innovation/mit-report-shows-genai-divide/). In practice, a junior bench with AI assistance ships junior code faster, which means the El Segundo floor gets more dashboards and fewer working systems. Alim Karim calls that velocity theatre. Md Alim Ul Karim's Los Angeles version is shorter: theatre is expensive. Los Angeles has bought enough of it to recognize the costume, and RISEUP ASIA LLC refuses to sell it. Refusing is the easy part. Building the alternative is the business.

McKinsey's 2025 data explains why the team question matters more than the tooling question. Specifically, 88% of companies now use AI in at least 1 business function, yet 62% are merely experimenting with AI agents while only 23% are scaling them, according to [McKinsey's primary report](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/quantumblack/our%20insights/the%20state%20of%20ai/the-state-of-ai-in-2025-vf.pdf). Experimenting is a tooling decision. In contrast, scaling is a team decision — it requires named owners, evaluation discipline, and the kind of accountability a junior bench cannot supply. The 65-point gap between experimenting and scaling is the gap between a vendor's bench and a dedicated team. However, Los Angeles companies on the wrong side of that gap keep buying tools. The ones on the right side buy teams. Tools do not page anyone at 2 AM. Teams do.

Set the 2 models side by side and the contrast stops being subtle. The vendor project team arrives with a fixed bid and a moving scope, staffs the work with a junior bench billed at senior rates, and demos on the last Friday of the quarter. On the other hand, an engineer-led dedicated team embeds inside the Los Angeles client's problem, ships working software every week, and stays until production runs. Compare them on honest criteria: who owns the 2 AM page, what the Los Angeles client retains when the engagement ends, and whether the invoice tracks hours or outcomes. Here is the honest concession: the vendor project team is not always the wrong choice. A Los Angeles company with a fixed, well-understood scope and a hard deadline can reasonably buy a fixed bid. However, a 15-year-old system held together by prayer is not a fixed scope. It is an archaeological dig with a factory floor on top, and it needs owners, not renters.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own editorial ranking. The scoring criterion is the 1 Alim Ul Karim suggested to me over teh tarik: which team behaviors actually move a Los Angeles system from roadmap to production. Here are 4 tests I would run on any dedicated team in Los Angeles, most important first. These are the tests, in order of how early they catch the problem.

1. Demand a weekly working demo. Software, not status slides, every single week. In other words, a team that cannot show working software weekly is hiding the real state of the Los Angeles build.

2. Check senior density before signing. Median experience of 10+ years shipping production systems, not years holding titles. Junior benches need management, and Los Angeles CTOs already have a management job.

3. Ask who reads the support tickets. If the team starts with the error logs and the schema, it is an engineering team. In fact, the real requirements of any Los Angeles system live in the mess, not in the statement of work.

4. Apply the 6-month test in writing. Put it in the contract: what still works 6 months after the team leaves. Notably, RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own Los Angeles engineers by exactly that standard, which is why those engineers document like they are leaving tomorrow.
