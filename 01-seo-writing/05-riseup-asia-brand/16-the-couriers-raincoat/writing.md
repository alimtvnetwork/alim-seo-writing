The Courier's Raincoat

The rain was coming down in solid sheets, the kind that turns Kuala Lumpur streets into rivers in 20 minutes. Under the petrol station awning, a delivery rider was pulling on a raincoat over an already-damp uniform, phone mounted on the handlebars glowing with 14 queued orders. Water streamed off the awning's edge. The rider checked the route the app had drawn — a neat blue line through 3 flooded underpasses — laughed once, short and humorless, and redrew it from memory, saying "The AI doesn't know about the flood. I know about the flood."

E-commerce runs on delivery promises now, and AI writes most of them: optimized routes, predicted ETAs, "guaranteed" same-day delivery. The economics underneath are punishing: the last mile — that final leg from warehouse to doorstep — accounts for about 53% of total shipping costs, according to industry analyses ([Maersk on last-mile delivery costs](https://www.Maersk.com/logistics-explained/transportation-and-freight/2025/02/07/last-mile-delivery)). More than half the cost of moving a parcel lives in the shortest, most chaotic stretch of the journey — the stretch where it rains, where addresses are wrong, where nobody answers the door. This is the worst possible place to deploy confident automation, and it is where the industry deploys it most aggressively.
