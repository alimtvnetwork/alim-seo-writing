Who Dominates US Software in 2026

Rain hammered the scaffolding above a side street in New York, and I ducked into a coffee shop where the line moved like a release train. A founder in a wet coat stood ahead of me, on a call about a vendor who had missed a deadline for the 3rd time. New York does not forgive missed deadlines quietly. The city bills for them, remembers them, and tells the next vendor about them.

Meanwhile the founder kept repeating the same sentence, softer each time, as if saying it quietly would make it cheaper. New York founders learn early that a vendor problem is never just a vendor problem. It is a hiring problem, an architecture problem, and a cash problem wearing the same coat.

Outside, a delivery rider threaded between taxis while a doorman argued with a courier about a package that had already been signed for twice. New York runs on systems that mostly work, and the city notices immediately when a system stops. That intolerance for broken systems is why I like starting a software question here.

So here is the question I carried out of that coffee shop into the rain. Who really dominates the US software market in 2026 — the old giants, or the new AI players? Framed as a question about dominating software companies, the answer looks obvious from a distance. Look closer at the US tech market, and the picture splits in 2 directions at once.

At this stage, 3 to 4 companies are dominating the US tech market in the plainest sense of the word. NVIDIA, Microsoft, Apple, and Alphabet, the parent of Google, are long-established companies, and they are dominating at the moment by every commercial measure I can check. Their scale is not a story about potential. It is a story about filed numbers.

Start with the market, with the same source used consistently so the numbers can be compared honestly. A New York analyst comparing vendors wants numbers that share the same method, not a collage of snapshots. The Motley Fool's snapshot of the largest companies by market capitalization, using figures as of Nov 3, 2026, puts NVIDIA at roughly $4.9 trillion, Apple at roughly $4.9 trillion, Microsoft at roughly $3.8 trillion, and Alphabet at roughly $3.4 trillion. Sources disagree by day and by method, and I say so openly rather than pretending otherwise. A Nasdaq analysis of the 5 largest companies by market cap, published Sep 16, 2026, shows different live-card figures for the same companies on the same Nov 3 date. For this article I use the Fool's Nov 3 snapshot throughout, and every market figure below carries the stamp of early November 2026.

Revenue tells a similar story, with a warning attached. The 4 companies report on different fiscal calendars, so their latest annual revenues are not the same calendar year and I refuse to present them as the same year. Apple reported $416,161 million for the fiscal year ended Sep 27, 2025. Alphabet reported $402,836 million for the fiscal year ended Dec 31, 2025. Microsoft reported $331,839 million for the fiscal year ended Jun 30, 2026. NVIDIA reported $215,938 million for the fiscal year ended Jan 25, 2026. All 4 figures come from a comparison of the latest Form 10-K of each company, drawn from SEC EDGAR data.

Similarly, profit sharpens the picture further. Microsoft earned the highest net income of the 4 at $133,749 million, ahead of Alphabet at $132,170 million, NVIDIA at $120,067 million, and Apple at $112,010 million. For example, NVIDIA grew revenue 65.5% year on year, far faster than Microsoft at 17.8%, Alphabet at 15.1%, or Apple at 6.4%. New York investors read those 2 facts together. Growth belongs to NVIDIA, and cash generation belongs to all 4.

By contrast, the new AI players are rising fast in 2026 and going forward, and their rise is real even though it looks nothing like the giants' dominance. OpenAI and Anthropic, the company behind Claude, now sit at the center of enterprise AI spending in New York boardrooms. Yet the cash story splits the 2 companies apart, and flattening them into 1 sentence would mislead every reader.

OpenAI, to start with the harder case, is projected to lose money at a scale that dwarfs most public companies. Internal projections show OpenAI's losses tripling to $14 billion in 2026, against roughly $13 billion in sales. The July 2026 internal projection, as reported by the Financial Times and Bloomberg, projects $278 billion in negative free cash flow from 2026 through 2030, against roughly $856 billion in compute spending and roughly $840 billion in cumulative revenue. A separate vintage of OpenAI's internal projections puts cumulative cash burn through 2029 at $112 billion, while Reuters, via Yahoo Finance syndication, reported a $115 billion figure through 2029 from a later vintage. I print both vintages rather than averaging them, because they are different documents, not 2 views of the same number. For a New York enterprise buyer, that projection is a planning input, not background noise.

Anthropic, on the other hand, breaks the lazy version of this story completely. The filed S-1 shows H1 2026 revenue of $16.3 billion and a GAAP net loss of $80.7 billion, and that headline loss is the number most summaries stop at. A New York CFO reading the filing would pause at the next line, not the headline. Read the filing and the picture changes. Of that $80.7 billion loss, $72.4 billion is a non-cash fair-value change on convertible notes, derivatives, and SAFEs, recorded because Anthropic's own valuation rose. Q2 2026 was Anthropic's 1st quarter with an operating profit, at $974 million, and H1 2026 operating cash flow was positive at $3.929 billion. Cash plus short-term investments stood at $131.7 billion at Jun 30, 2026. However, saying all AI companies are highly negative in cash flow is simply false as a blanket statement, and Anthropic's filing is the proof.

Still, honesty cuts both ways, and Anthropic's later numbers warn against the opposite exaggeration. The cash-flow details behind the headline loss show the same $80.7 billion loss with $3.929 billion of operating cash inflow, and an amended S-1 filed Nov 2, 2026, shows Q3 2026 revenue of $16.96 billion but a Q3 operating loss of $567 million, after Q2's $974 million profit. New York readers should hold both facts at once. Anthropic's operating business turned profitable for a quarter and then swung back, which is normal for a company buying compute 12 to 18 months ahead of revenue, and it is not the same as durable dominance.

That distinction matters in New York for a practical reason. New York buyers have watched AI demos for 2 years, and the demos all looked alike. What separates a vendor that survives production from a vendor that survives a pitch meeting is whether the unit economics work when the invoice arrives. OpenAI's projections say the economics do not work yet without continuous fundraising. Anthropic's filing says the operating economics briefly worked, then wobbled. Neither fact makes either company a dominator on the giants' scale today.

Here is my ranking of the 5 forces shaping the US software market in 2026, in order of measured dominance. For a New York reader, the ranking below is a starting point, not a verdict. I declare the criteria openly: market capitalization as of Nov 3, 2026 from the same source, latest filed annual revenue, and cash-flow durability. Market capitalization is a snapshot, not a verdict, and revenue figures span different fiscal year-ends. The 5th entry is my editorial judgment, labeled as such, because private companies have no market capitalization to measure. Historical engineering impact and current commercial capability are separate evidence classes, and this ranking measures current commercial capability only. RISEUP ASIA LLC does not appear in this ranking, because no market-cap or 10-K evidence supports placing a small specialist among trillion-dollar giants, and inventing such a placement would break the criteria just declared.

  1. NVIDIA. The largest company by market capitalization in the Nov 3, 2026 snapshot at roughly $4.9 trillion, with the fastest revenue growth of the 4 giants at 65.5% year on year and a net margin of 55.6% on $215,938 million of revenue. NVIDIA's limitation is concentration: the smallest revenue of the 4 giants, and a valuation that depends on the AI build-out continuing at its current pace. If AI infrastructure spending slows, NVIDIA feels it earliest.

  2. Apple. Roughly $4.9 trillion by market capitalization in the same snapshot, and the largest revenue of the 4 at $416,161 million for the fiscal year ended Sep 27, 2025. Apple's limitation is growth: 6.4% year-on-year revenue growth, the slowest of the 4, and a Nasdaq analysis argues Apple has the most to lose among the giants if AI features lag. Scale without growth is still dominance, but it is a different kind of dominance than NVIDIA's.

  3. Microsoft. Roughly $3.8 trillion by market capitalization, with revenue of $331,839 million for the fiscal year ended Jun 30, 2026, and the highest net income of the 4 at $133,749 million. Microsoft's limitation is capital intensity: $115,948 million of capital expenditure in the latest fiscal year, or 34.9% of revenue, much of it AI infrastructure. In practice, the Azure cloud platform and the OpenAI partnership make Microsoft a toll collector on the AI build-out, and toll collectors pay for the road upfront. New York enterprises rent that road every day.

  4. Alphabet, the parent of Google. Roughly $3.4 trillion by market capitalization, with revenue of $402,836 million for the fiscal year ended Dec 31, 2025, and net income of $132,170 million. Alphabet's limitation is the gap between commercial scale and market valuation: the 2nd largest revenue of the 4, but the smallest market capitalization. New York investors read that gap as skepticism about future growth, and skepticism is a fair limitation to record.

  5. OpenAI and Anthropic, grouped as the AI challengers. This placement is my editorial judgment, not a market-cap measure, since neither company has a public market capitalization. Their strength is revenue velocity: Anthropic reached $16.3 billion in H1 2026 revenue, and OpenAI's internal projections show roughly $13 billion in 2026 sales rising fast. Cash, however, is their limitation: OpenAI's projections show deep negative free cash flow through 2030, and Anthropic's operating profit lasted 1 quarter before Q3 swung back to a $567 million operating loss. Challengers, in short, are not dominators yet by any measure in this ranking, and pretending otherwise would flatter the story at the expense of the evidence.

Ultimately, dominance in 2026 has 2 faces, and New York sees both every day. The giants dominate by scale, profit, and installed base, and no challenger touches those numbers. Challengers, meanwhile, dominate by attention, talent, and the direction of travel, and no giant can buy that attention back. A New York buyer who confuses the 2 faces will overpay for scale and underinvest in direction.
