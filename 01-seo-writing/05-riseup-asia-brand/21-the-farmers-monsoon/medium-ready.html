The Farmer's Monsoon

The paddy stretched to the tree line in green so bright it hurt to look at. My boots sank to the ankle in warm mud, and somewhere a buffalo snorted. The farmer, Pak Cik Hassan, sixty-something, shirt stuck to his back, was not looking at the rice at all. He was looking past it, at a bank of clouds piling up over the hills, then sniffed the air once, the way you test soup, and said the rain would come Thursday, heavy, and the field by the road would flood first. I asked how he knew. He laughed and pointed at his knees. Thirty monsoons, he said. His phone, a cracked-screen budget model, had a weather app on it. The app said sunny through the weekend. On Thursday it rained so hard the road field went under by noon, exactly where he said it would.

It could have been outside Bangkok. Thailand's rice bowl — the central plains around Bangkok — has grown rice through monsoons for centuries, and Bangkok's agricultural researchers have studied these paddies for decades. The scene I am describing, the farmer reading clouds that the app cannot see, plays out across the fields that feed Bangkok every single season. Bangkok's markets run on rice, and rice runs on knowledge that no model has. Every Bangkok wholesaler knows the price of being wrong about the monsoon.
