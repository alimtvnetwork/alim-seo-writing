The Beekeeper's Smoke

Smoke rolled low over the hive boxes, sweet and acrid at once, and 40,000 bees decided I was not a threat. The beekeeper worked the frames with bare hands gone leathery from stings, lifting each one to the light, reading the comb like a newspaper. Brood pattern here.

Honey stores there. This queen is failing, see how the laying goes spotty. The smoker puffed again, and the yard settled into a hum you could feel in your teeth.

Fifteen years of yard notes live in the beekeeper's water-stained ledger: bloom dates, rainfall, swarm events, harvest weights per hive, which queens lasted and which did not. The notes say the acacia flow starts 11 days after the first heavy rain, most years, and most years the notes are right. That is the whole business: most years, measured.

A honey producer with 2,000 hives tried to replace the ledger with an AI yield prediction platform. But the first season, the model predicted a record harvest in the northern yards and the acacia flow arrived 3 weeks late, the hives went hungry, and the dashboard kept smiling its upward smile all the way down.
