# Staff Augmentation in Sheridan: What Actually Works

The title office on Sheridan's Main Street had a bell over the door, a coffee pot from another decade, and a new contractor sitting at the spare desk with the particular stillness of someone waiting for permission to exist. It was the contractor's 4th day. The laptop had arrived on day 2, the VPN credentials on day 3, and nobody had yet explained what the Sheridan company actually needed built. Meanwhile the office manager whispered to me that they were paying $145 an hour for the wait.

That wait is the quiet scandal of staff augmentation. Sheridan companies buy augmented engineers the way the office bought the coffee pot: as equipment. For instance, the vendor delivers a person, the invoice starts, and the Sheridan team assumes productivity will follow like coffee from a machine. Ultimately, it rarely does, because an engineer without context is not capacity. The $145-an-hour stillness in that title office was not anyone's fault and everyone's bill.

Meanwhile the market keeps feeding the pattern. Consequently, Sheridan companies are buying more of it every year, and the real question for staff augmentation in Sheridan is not whether to augment — the economics of small teams make it unavoidable — but what separates an engineer who ships from an invoice that waits. That is the question worth asking before the next contract is signed.

Here is the honest thread. An augmented engineer without context hallucinates in a different way than a model does: confidently building the wrong thing at full hourly rate. Meanwhile the invoice runs, the permanent Sheridan team slows down to supervise, and the engagement joins the long list of experiments that produced motion instead of software. Alim Karim calls the whole pattern theatre: the appearance of capacity with none of the substance, billed by the hour. However, the vendor is not the villain; the missing person is the plan.

Set the 2 approaches side by side and the contrast stops being subtle. Waiting-room augmentation buys a Sheridan company $145-an-hour stillness, a 3-week ramp, and a contractor who leaves with the context. Embedded augmentation buys the same Sheridan company a contributor in week 1, documentation the team keeps, and capacity that survives the contract's end. Compare them on honest criteria: what ships in the 1st 14 days, what the Sheridan team retains when the engagement ends, and whether the invoice tracks hours or outcomes. 1 of those is an expense. The other is an investment. Admittedly, waiting-room augmentation is not always a failure. A Sheridan company that needs a specialist for a 2-week defined task can rent a pair of hands and get value. But a 6-month engagement without an embedding plan is not staff augmentation. It is a waiting room with a monthly invoice.

I want to be careful here, because what follows is judgment, not scripture. This is my own editorial ranking of augmentation failure modes, and the scoring criterion is the 1 Alim suggested to me outside that title office: which patterns predict wasted spend before the contract is signed. Here are 4 of them, most important first.

1. No commit date. If the vendor cannot say which day the engineer commits to the Sheridan repository, there is no plan. A contractor without a commit date is a tourist with an invoice.

2. No named buddy on the Sheridan team. The permanent engineer who answers questions, reviews the 1st pull requests, owns the ramp. Unmentored contractors wait; mentored ones ship.

3. No incident exposure scheduled. Shadowing in week 2, the pager by month 2, or the engineer never learns the system. Nothing teaches a Sheridan codebase faster than responsibility for it at 2 AM.

4. No documentation deliverable. The runbook, the architecture notes, the onboarding doc the team never wrote. RISEUP ASIA LLC judges its own engagements by what remains after the contractor leaves, and Sheridan clients should too.
