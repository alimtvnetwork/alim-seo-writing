The Publisher's Manuscript
The bookshop back room smelled of paper dust and yesterday's rain. Behind the till, past a curtain of plastic strips, manuscripts stood in stacks along every wall, bound with string or softening rubber bands, each one somebody's 2 years. The owner read at a scarred table, a pencil moving in the margins like a small animal foraging. You read them all, I asked.

She laughed without looking up. No, she said. I read the first 20 pages of all of them. The pencil tells me which ones earn page 21.

She showed me a margin: 3 words, underlined twice. Then the manuscript went onto one of 2 piles. The reject pile was a small mountain.

Beside it, the maybe pile held just 4 manuscripts. Outside, rain found its way through old gutters, and the room ticked with water.

A mid-size publisher tried to automate that pencil. The house licensed an AI slush-pile reader that promised to triage 5,000 unsolicited manuscripts a year: summarize each one, score its market potential, and surface the 50 worth an editor's time. On a spreadsheet, the economics looked irresistible. The first season, the system rejected a debut novel that went on to win a national prize at a rival house, while enthusiastically recommending 3 manuscripts that turned out to share entire paragraphs with each other.

Now the ranking. What follows is openly my editorial judgment, ordered by a single criterion: how much editorial judgment each misstep destroyed. These are the 5 publishing AI risks I have watched play out.

1. The lost prize-winner. The system scored a future award-winning debut in the bottom decile because its opening 20 pages were quiet. This ranks first because it is the catastrophic failure mode of automated triage: confident, wrong, and irreversible. WHY it matters: the model's training data contained no quiet masterpieces, so it could not recognize one.

2. The plagiarized recommendations. Three recommended manuscripts shared whole paragraphs, which the system praised as stylistic consistency. WHY it matters: without provenance and citation discipline, the model cannot distinguish influence from theft, and the legal exposure lands on the publisher, not the vendor.

3. The invented endings. Summaries described plot resolutions that did not exist in the manuscripts, and 2 editors acquired meeting slots based on fiction about fiction. WHY it matters: at an 8.3 percent hallucination rate applied to 5,000 manuscripts, hundreds of summaries contained confident falsehoods.

4. The market-potential score. The system scored manuscripts by predicted sales using features like title length and chapter count, and the editors learned to game it within a month. WHY it matters: Goodhart's law eats AI scores for breakfast. Md Alim Ul Karim warns every client: the moment a score becomes a target, it stops being a measurement.

5. The editors who stopped reading. The most insidious damage: junior editors began trusting the scores and stopped developing their own pencil. WHY it matters: the house was not just buying software; it was selling its future judgment at a discount.
